<b>Research date:</b> 13 November 2026<br /><b>Profile considered:</b> 2026 [B.Tech](http://B.Tech) CSE graduate, based in Vijayawada, open to relocating anywhere in India; interested in AI/ML and software engineering; portfolio includes AgentForge and DocWeave.

## Executive conclusion

The Indian technology market is not uniformly bad. It is highly segmented. There are many experienced-hire openings, fewer true fresher openings, and a wide compensation spread because employers pay for different levels of business impact, scarcity, responsibility, and interview performance. A high package is an outlier or a specific employer/role outcome—not the standard value of a [B.Tech](http://B.Tech) degree.

<b>Recommended strategy:</b> pursue two tracks simultaneously:

1. <b>Primary career track:</b> software/backend engineering, AI application/GenAI engineering, and applied AI roles that match the existing portfolio.
2. <b>Stretch track:</b> ML engineering, AI/ML research internships, research engineering, and foundation-model roles when the job description genuinely accepts fresh graduates.

Do not restrict the search to “AI Engineer” titles. Search for the work described in the job description.

## Why [B.Tech](http://B.Tech) salaries vary so much

A degree does not have one fixed market price. Compensation is set by the employer's economics and the role's expected impact.

1. <b>Employer type:</b> IT services, startups, product companies, and global capability centres have different revenue models and pay bands.
2. <b>Role and scarcity:</b> a general entry-level developer role and a specialist infrastructure/ML role are not priced identically.
3. <b>Selection funnel:</b> highly selective companies hire very few graduates and can pay much more to those who clear their interviews.
4. <b>Business impact:</b> engineers working on revenue-critical products, core infrastructure, or high-value systems can command more.
5. <b>Location and work model:</b> metro pay bands, on-site requirements, and relocation affect compensation and take-home value.
6. <b>CTC composition:</b> headline CTC may include variable pay, joining bonuses, stock/ESOPs, and benefits; it is not the same as monthly fixed salary.

The doctor comparison is understandable but not exact. Doctors' earnings also vary by specialty, hospital, city, seniority, government/private employment, and private practice. Salaried hospital jobs may look more standardised than technology compensation, but medicine is not one fixed salary ladder either.

For the next 4–6 weeks, use a portfolio approach rather than betting everything on one title:

# Deep-dive research: How companies make money, why they hire, and why compensation differs

<b>Research update:</b> 13 November 2026<br /><b>Scope:</b> Global business models, with an India technology-industry focus. This section explains company economics before drawing conclusions about jobs and salaries.

## 1. Why companies exist

A company coordinates people, capital, equipment, intellectual property, suppliers, and processes to deliver a product or service that customers value. Customers pay because the offering solves a problem, saves time or money, reduces risk, or provides an experience they want.

A company is not necessarily a factory or software office. It can be a bank, marketplace, cloud platform, consultancy, chip designer, hospital, logistics network, media business, or manufacturer. Most commercial companies seek sustainable profit; some organizations instead prioritize public, cooperative, or nonprofit objectives.

The basic economic cycle is:

1. Identify a customer problem or unmet need.
2. Create an offering and a way to deliver it.
3. Acquire customers and collect revenue.
4. Pay for labour, materials, technology, capital, distribution, taxes, and risk.
5. Reinvest, repay investors/lenders, hold reserves, or distribute remaining profit.

<b>Key distinction:</b> revenue is not profit, and profit is not cash available to spend on hiring. Working capital, debt, capital expenditure, taxes, and future commitments also matter.

## 2. The main ways companies earn revenue

| Business model | Who pays, and for what? | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| IT services / consulting | A client pays for people, expertise, project delivery, managed services, or an agreed outcome. Contracts may be time-and-material, fixed-price, or outcome-based. | TCS, Infosys, Accenture |
| Software product / SaaS | Customers pay subscription fees, licences, usage fees, or enterprise contracts for software. | Microsoft, Adobe, Salesforce |
| Advertising | Advertisers pay to reach audiences or generate measurable actions; users may use the service free. | Google, Meta |
| Marketplace / platform | Buyers and sellers transact; the platform may collect a commission, listing fee, payment fee, or other service revenue. | Amazon Marketplace, Uber, Airbnb |
| Financial services | Customers pay interest, fees, premiums, asset-management fees, or transaction-related charges; firms also manage credit, market, and regulatory risk. | Banks, insurers, brokerages |
| Trading / market making | A firm deploys capital and models to trade or provide liquidity, aiming to earn spreads or trading returns. Profits are uncertain and can be negative. | Quantitative trading firms |
| Hardware / semiconductor | Revenue comes from selling chips, devices, systems, components, or licensing intellectual property. | NVIDIA, Qualcomm, Arm |
| Manufacturing / industrial | Customers pay for physical goods, equipment, components, and sometimes maintenance or service contracts. | Tata Motors, Siemens |
| Telecom / cloud / infrastructure | Customers pay recurring access, capacity, data, compute, storage, connectivity, or usage charges. | AWS, Azure, telecom operators |
| Media / content / gaming | Revenue may come from subscriptions, advertising, licences, in-app purchases, or transactions. | Streaming, game publishers |
| Healthcare / life sciences | Revenue comes from services, medicines, devices, diagnostics, insurance reimbursement, and research partnerships. | Hospitals, pharma, medtech |

A company may combine several models. For example, a software company can sell subscriptions, charge for usage, offer consulting, and earn marketplace fees.

## 3. How the models work in practice

### A. IT services: selling expertise and delivery

A client has a business problem: modernize a bank's application, migrate data to cloud, test software, secure infrastructure, or operate a customer-support process. The services provider signs a contract and supplies a team and delivery capability.

Common contract structures:

- <b>Time and materials:</b> the client pays for agreed effort, roles, hours, or units delivered.
- <b>Fixed price:</b> the provider agrees to deliver a defined scope for a negotiated amount; overruns can reduce its margin.
- <b>Managed services:</b> the provider operates a service for a recurring fee, often against service-level commitments.
- <b>Outcome-based:</b> payment is linked to agreed results or performance measures.

TCS's FY2024–25 annual-report materials describe revenue from IT services, consulting and business solutions, including time-and-material and fixed-price recognition methods. This illustrates how a large Indian services provider earns and recognises contract revenue. [TCS Integrated Annual Report 2024–25](https://www.tcs.com/content/dam/tcs/pdf/discover-tcs/investor-relations/faq/tcs-integrated-annual-report-2024-2025-2pager.pdf).

<b>Hiring implication:</b> services firms need people who can deliver client work at a predictable cost, quality, and schedule. Utilisation, billable work, project demand, delivery risk, and client budgets influence hiring and pay.

### B. Product and SaaS: building once, selling repeatedly

A company develops software and sells access to many customers. Revenue may recur monthly or annually, or depend on usage. The economics can be attractive because the next customer may not require a completely new product build, although support, sales, cloud infrastructure, security, and continued development still cost money.

<b>Hiring implication:</b> engineers may work on a shared product used by many customers. Reliability, security, user experience, scalability, retention, and revenue growth can be strategically important. Product companies still vary widely in size, margins, maturity, and pay.

### C. Advertising: users create attention, advertisers fund the service

A social or search platform attracts users and earns money by selling advertising opportunities. Advertisers may pay per impression, click, or other action. Meta's 2025 Form 10-K states that substantially all its revenue came from advertising; it reported 2025 advertising revenue of about US\$196.2 billion. [Meta 2025 Form 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1326801/000162828026025534/meta-12312025x10kars.htm).

<b>Hiring implication:</b> ranking, recommendation, ad delivery, measurement, experimentation, privacy, data infrastructure, and reliability can directly affect revenue. A software change may influence billions of ad impressions, but impact must be demonstrated rather than assumed.

### D. Marketplaces: connecting two sides

A marketplace connects buyers and sellers, riders and drivers, hosts and guests, or service providers and customers. It may charge a transaction fee or commission. The platform's value is not just software: it must create trust, liquidity, matching, payments, dispute resolution, and sufficient supply and demand.

<b>Hiring implication:</b> engineers may work on search and matching, fraud prevention, payments, pricing, logistics, identity, and marketplace reliability. A small technical failure can affect many transactions.

### E. Financial services and quantitative trading

Banks and insurers earn through a mix of interest spreads, fees, premiums, underwriting, and investment-related activities. Quantitative trading firms use models, data, software, and capital to trade or provide liquidity. These businesses face market, credit, operational, legal, and regulatory risks.

<b>Hiring implication:</b> a quantitative developer may need probability, statistics, algorithms, C++, distributed systems, and low-latency engineering. High compensation at some firms reflects a selective market and potentially high economic contribution, but trading profits are volatile and high pay is not guaranteed.

### F. Semiconductors and intellectual property

Some semiconductor companies design chips and outsource fabrication; others manufacture chips or sell equipment. Some firms license designs and collect royalties. Qualcomm describes its semiconductor products and intellectual-property licensing businesses in its SEC filing; Arm describes licensing IP and receiving per-chip royalties in its FY2026 filing. [Qualcomm FY2025 Form 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/804328/000080432825000085/qcom-20250928.htm) · [Arm FY2026 Form 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1973239/000197323926000114/arm-20260630.htm).

<b>Hiring implication:</b> chip design, verification, compilers, architecture, embedded software, physical design, and manufacturing process expertise are specialised. CSE graduates can enter some software, systems, compiler, and architecture roles, while other roles require electronics or semiconductor-specific foundations.

## 4. Why companies hire employees instead of buying everything externally

A company hires when it believes an employee or team is a useful way to obtain capability, control, speed, continuity, or intellectual property.

Common reasons:

- <b>Build:</b> create a new product, feature, or internal capability.
- <b>Operate:</b> keep existing systems, factories, networks, and services running.
- <b>Improve:</b> increase revenue, conversion, quality, speed, or productivity.
- <b>Protect:</b> reduce security, fraud, compliance, and operational risks.
- <b>Scale:</b> serve more customers, markets, transactions, and workloads.
- <b>Innovate:</b> research and test new products, algorithms, materials, or business models.
- <b>Support customers:</b> sell, implement, train, troubleshoot, and retain customers.

Hiring is not simply a reward for having a degree. It is an investment decision: does the expected contribution of this role justify its full cost and risk compared with alternatives such as automation, outsourcing, software purchases, or doing nothing?

## 5. Why the same degree can lead to very different salaries

Compensation is shaped by several interacting forces:

1. <b>Ability to pay:</b> revenue, margins, cash flow, funding, and budget.
2. <b>Value of the work:</b> whether the role influences revenue, cost, risk, customer retention, or strategic capability.
3. <b>Scarcity:</b> how many candidates can perform the work at the required level.
4. <b>Substitutability:</b> how easily the company can hire another person, outsource the work, or automate it.
5. <b>Scale and leverage:</b> whether the output of one engineer can benefit a small team, millions of users, or a global platform.
6. <b>Responsibility and risk:</b> the cost of failure, security exposure, compliance, uptime, and customer impact.
7. <b>Hiring selectivity:</b> number of applicants, interview bar, and the employer's willingness to compete for a small talent pool.
8. <b>Geography:</b> local labour market, currency, tax, cost of living, and whether the role is globally or locally benchmarked.
9. <b>Experience and evidence:</b> ability to deliver in production, not just familiarity with tools.
10. <b>Negotiation and alternatives:</b> competing offers and the candidate's bargaining power.
11. <b>Timing:</b> funding cycles, technology shifts, hiring freezes, and business demand.
12. <b>Compensation design:</b> fixed pay, variable pay, joining bonus, RSUs, ESOPs, benefits, and vesting conditions.

No single factor explains every salary. The same company can pay very different amounts to different job families, levels, locations, and candidates.

## 6. A crucial correction: salary is not a direct share of the money an employee generates

An engineer's output is often a team outcome. Revenue attribution is difficult. A company also needs to retain capital, pay for infrastructure and sales, manage risk, and compete with other employers.

A more accurate model is:

<b>The employer offers compensation based on the expected value of the role, the cost of alternatives, the available budget, and the market price needed to attract and retain a suitable person.</b>

This is why a high-revenue company does not automatically pay every employee a high salary, and why a small company may sometimes pay a premium for one scarce specialist.

## 7. India technology economy: scale and structure

The latest available FY2026 estimates show India's IT and BPM industry at approximately US\$315 billion in revenue, with exports contributing about US\$246.4 billion in the cited February 2026 estimate. IBEF's September 2026 industry page summarises these figures and identifies IT services as a major export contributor. [IBEF: IT & BPM Industry in India](https://www.ibef.org/industry/information-technology-india).

The India GCC landscape report by Zinnov and Nasscom reports 2,117 GCCs, US\$98.4 billion in revenue, and approximately 2.36 million professionals in FY2026. It describes a shift from delivery centres toward greater ownership of products, platforms, and enterprise capabilities. [Zinnov–Nasscom GCC Landscape 2026](https://zinnov.com/centers-of-excellence/zinnov-nasscom-india-gcc-landscape-2026-report/).

Nasscom–Zinnov's 2026 startup report says Indian tech startups raised US\$9.1 billion in 2025, up 23% year-on-year, while investors became more selective about scalable business models and commercialisation. [India Tech Startup Report 2026](https://d3r3sr3o54kk15.cloudfront.net/s3fs-public/media_pdf/Nasscom%20%E2%80%93%20Zinnov%20Tech%20Start-up%20Report%202026_India%E2%80%99s%20Tech%20Start-ups%20at%20a%20Strategic%20Inflection%20Point%20as%20Funding%20Rises%20to%20%249.1%20Bn.pdf).

These figures show that India is not one homogeneous IT labour market. It contains services exporters, domestic technology businesses, global capability centres, product companies, startups, hardware and engineering research, and financial technology employers.

## 8. How this connects to the salary examples

| Example employer / role | Economic logic behind possible pay differences |
| --- | --- |
| IT services fresher | Delivery economics, project pricing, utilisation, standardised entry-level bands |
| Product-company software engineer | Shared product leverage, customer retention, reliability, growth, and competitive hiring |
| GCC engineer | Global parent-company budget, India talent strategy, local compensation bands, ownership of global systems |

These are explanations of possible compensation mechanisms, not claims that every employer in a category pays the same way.

## 9. What headline CTC does and does not mean

Always separate:

- Fixed annual base salary.
- Target or guaranteed variable pay.
- Joining and retention bonuses.
- RSUs or other listed-company equity, including vesting schedule and share-price risk.
- Startup ESOPs, including strike price, dilution, exercise cost, and liquidity uncertainty.
- Benefits and employer contributions.

A headline CTC of ₹X is not necessarily ₹X in cash received during the year. Compare fixed pay and realistic cash first; value equity separately and conservatively.

## 10. What this means for a 2026 [B.Tech](http://B.Tech) CSE graduate

The degree is a starting signal, not a salary category. The practical task is to identify which business problems you can credibly solve and which employers need those capabilities.

For an early-career CSE candidate interested in backend and applied GenAI:

- Services companies can offer a broad entry route into client engineering and production delivery.
- Product companies can offer work on shared software platforms and customer-facing systems.
- GCCs can offer ownership of global enterprise products, data, cloud, and AI systems.
- Startups can offer broad responsibility, but carry greater business and funding uncertainty.
- AI infrastructure, research, and quantitative roles can be attractive stretch paths where the required mathematical and systems foundations match the role.

A sensible first-job comparison should consider learning, engineering ownership, mentorship, team quality, stability, fixed pay, location, and future mobility—not headline CTC alone.

## 11. Evidence and limitations

- Industry revenue figures are estimates and use different definitions and reporting dates. Do not add overlapping categories.
- GCC figures describe an ecosystem, not the number of current vacancies.
- Startup funding is not revenue, profit, or a guarantee of hiring.
- Public annual reports explain business models and reported financials, but do not reveal every role's salary budget.
- There is no reliable public dataset that maps every Indian [B.Tech](http://B.Tech) graduate to a complete, verified salary distribution from ₹3 LPA to ₹2 crore. Exceptional placement announcements are not representative of all graduates.
- The business-model examples are simplified; actual firms may combine multiple models and have different segment economics.

# Book project charter and research architecture

## Working title

<b>The Economics of Jobs: How Companies, Technology, Markets, and Skills Determine Careers and Salaries</b>

Subtitle: <b>A first-principles guide to the global employment system, with a focus on Indian engineering graduates</b>

## 1. The user's original question — holistically framed

A person has completed a [B.Tech](http://B.Tech) degree in Computer Science or another engineering branch. Across the labour market, some graduates receive offers around ₹3 LPA, others ₹5–12 LPA, others ₹20–55 LPA, and a small number announce packages of ₹1 crore or more. Why can outcomes differ so dramatically even when the formal qualification appears similar?

The book will answer the full system behind that observation:

1. What is a company, why do firms exist, and how do they create and capture economic value?
2. What kinds of companies exist globally, and how do their business models, industries, customers, costs, revenues, risks, and profit structures differ?
3. Why do firms need workers, and how do they decide whether to hire, outsource, automate, train, or leave a role unfilled?
4. How are jobs created across industries, occupations, technologies, and geographies?
5. How does the labour market match workers to jobs? What roles do education, skills, experience, signalling, referrals, recruitment channels, and bargaining power play?
6. How do wages and total compensation get determined? How do productivity, scarcity, firm profitability, competition, monopsony, regulation, location, and negotiation interact?
7. Why are compensation distributions unequal and often highly skewed? What do mean, median, percentiles, outliers, and selection effects reveal?
8. Why do campus placements, off-campus hiring, elite institutions, startups, product companies, services firms, GCCs, financial firms, and research labs produce different outcomes?
9. How do technology shifts—AI, automation, cloud, semiconductors, robotics, and software platforms—change the demand for skills and the value of work?
10. What does this system look like in India compared with the United States, Europe, and other labour markets?
11. What can an individual graduate realistically control, what is structural or outside their control, and how should they make career decisions under uncertainty?

The central question is not merely "Why do salaries differ?" It is:

<b>How does the global economy transform customer demand, company strategy, technology, capital, and human capability into jobs, hiring decisions, and unequal compensation—and where does an engineering graduate fit into that system?</b>

## 2. The central explanatory model

Use this causal chain as the book's backbone:

<b>Human needs and customer demand → products and services → business models → company revenue and costs → investment and strategy → work to be done → job design and hiring → labour-market matching and selection → compensation offers → employee outcomes.</b>

This chain is not perfectly linear. Technology, regulation, competition, finance, global trade, worker bargaining power, and macroeconomic cycles feed back into multiple stages.

A salary is therefore not a direct price tag attached to a degree. It is an outcome of an employer's ability and willingness to pay, the value and risk associated with a role, the available alternatives, the supply of qualified candidates, and the bargaining process.

## 3. Research principles and quality rules

- Start with first principles and define technical/economic terms before using them.
- Separate facts, estimates, forecasts, interpretations, and hypotheses.
- Prefer primary sources: government datasets, labour surveys, company annual reports and SEC filings, official industry reports, academic papers, and reputable multilateral research.
- Use secondary reporting to discover or contextualise evidence, not as the sole support for important claims when primary evidence is available.
- Record publication date, data period, geography, population, denominator, definition, methodology, and limitations for every quantitative statistic.
- Never combine incompatible measures (for example, job-posting shares, unemployment rates, employability assessments, and hiring forecasts).
- Distinguish salary, fixed pay, cash compensation, variable pay, equity, CTC, and realised income.
- Do not infer that a high-paid worker is proportionally more intelligent or more valuable as a human being.
- Include counterexamples and competing explanations; do not reduce every wage difference to "the employee generates more revenue."
- Explain labour-market imperfections: employer market power, information asymmetry, discrimination, credential signalling, geographic constraints, search costs, and unequal access to opportunities.
- Keep an India lens without treating India as one uniform market; compare sectors, cities, firm types, and worker cohorts.

# Stage 1 — The Economic World

## Chapter 1, Part 1: Economics begins with scarcity

<b>Research status:</b> Part 1 draft completed; Stage 1 Parts 1–8 and the internal integrated review are now complete.<br /><b>Research date:</b> 2026-11-13<br /><b>Scope:</b> Global concepts, explained with everyday and Indian examples.

## 1. The starting point: what is economics?

Economics is not simply the study of money, stock markets, banks, or business. It is the study of how people and societies make choices when the resources available to them are limited relative to the different things they could do with those resources.

Resources include:

- Time and human attention.
- Knowledge, skills, and physical effort.
- Land, water, energy, and raw materials.
- Machines, buildings, tools, and infrastructure.
- Money and other financial resources.
- Technology and organisational capability.

OpenStax's introductory economics text frames economics around decisions under scarcity and explains that scarcity forces individuals, businesses, and societies to make choices. [OpenStax — What Is Economics?](https://openstax.org/books/principles-microeconomics-2e/pages/1-1-what-is-economics-and-why-is-it-important)

### A simple example

Suppose a student has four free hours today. The student could:

- Study programming for two hours.
- Prepare for an interview.
- Exercise.
- Work on a portfolio project.
- Rest or spend time with family.

The student cannot devote the same four hours fully to every option. The limitation is not necessarily money. The scarce resource here is time.

This is the first major insight: <b>economics is about allocating limited resources among competing uses.</b>

## 2. Scarcity does not mean poverty

These terms must not be confused.

- <b>Scarcity:</b> A resource is limited relative to the possible uses or wants for it. It applies to rich and poor people, firms, and governments.
- <b>Poverty:</b> A condition in which a person or household lacks sufficient resources to meet a defined standard of basic needs.
- <b>Shortage:</b> A situation in a particular market where the quantity demanded exceeds the quantity supplied at the prevailing price. A shortage can be temporary or caused by a price control, disruption, or other constraint.

A wealthy person still has only 24 hours in a day. A large technology company still has to choose how to allocate engineers, computing capacity, capital, and management attention. Scarcity is universal; poverty is not.

## 3. Needs, wants, and effective demand

In everyday speech, people distinguish needs from wants. This is useful, but economists should be precise about the context.

- A <b>need</b> is something considered necessary for a person's basic functioning or a chosen standard of life, such as food, shelter, or healthcare.
- A <b>want</b> is a desired good, service, experience, or outcome. Wants can extend far beyond basic needs.
- <b>Demand</b> in a market is not merely wanting something. It is willingness and ability to purchase a good or service at a given price over a given period.

Example: many people may want a premium laptop. But only those who are both willing and able to buy it at the offered price contribute to market demand at that price.

This distinction matters for companies. A company cannot build a sustainable business merely because people say they like an idea. It needs customers who are willing and able to pay, or another credible way to fund the service.

## 4. Choice creates trade-offs

When a resource has multiple possible uses, choosing one use means giving up some alternatives.

A trade-off is what must be sacrificed when choosing one option over another.

Example: if a graduate spends Saturday building a portfolio project, they may give up some interview preparation or leisure time. The project may be worthwhile, but the other uses of Saturday do not become free.

The same logic applies to a company:

- Spend money on hiring more engineers, or on better infrastructure?
- Launch a new product, or improve an existing product?
- Build a feature internally, or purchase a third-party service?
- Expand into another country, or strengthen the current market?

Economics studies these choices and their consequences.

## 5. Opportunity cost: the hidden cost of a decision

<b>Opportunity cost is the value of the best alternative forgone when a choice is made.</b>

It is not automatically the sum of every alternative that was not chosen. It is the value of the next-best option sacrificed.

Example:

- You have one free evening.
- Option A: prepare for a technical interview.
- Option B: complete a portfolio feature.
- Option C: watch a film.
- You choose B.
- If A was the best alternative you gave up, the opportunity cost is the value of the interview preparation you could have completed—not the combined value of A and C.

Opportunity cost can include:

- Money.
- Time.
- Income you could have earned.
- Learning or experience you could have gained.
- Risk or flexibility you give up.

OpenStax explicitly notes that opportunity cost can include time and other resources, not just money. [OpenStax — Choice and Opportunity Cost](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-2e/pages/2-key-concepts-and-summary)

### Why this concept matters to the book

A company choosing to hire one category of engineer gives up the ability to use that budget elsewhere. A graduate accepting one job may give up another role, additional study, or a different location. Neither decision can be understood only by looking at the money paid or received.

Opportunity cost will reappear when we study:

- Why firms hire or outsource.
- Why companies choose one technology over another.
- Why graduates choose one job offer over another.
- Why training and higher education involve economic trade-offs.

## 6. The three fundamental allocation questions

Every economic system must address three broad questions:

1. <b>What should be produced?</b> Which goods and services should receive scarce resources?
2. <b>How should it be produced?</b> Which combination of labour, machines, technology, energy, and methods should be used?
3. <b>For whom should it be produced?</b> Who receives the output, and how is access or purchasing power distributed?

Different societies answer these questions through different combinations of markets, firms, households, governments, customs, and public institutions. No real economy is simply one pure mechanism.

These questions connect directly to jobs:

- What is produced influences which industries and occupations are needed.
- How it is produced influences which tasks are performed by people, machines, or software.
- For whom it is produced influences customers, access, revenue, and the distribution of economic benefits.

## 7. First connection to employment and salary

At this stage we should not jump to the claim that a worker's salary equals the value they create. That would be an oversimplification.

For now, establish this sequence:

1. People and organisations have goals and wants.
2. Resources are limited.
3. They choose among competing uses.
4. Production requires resources and coordinated tasks.
5. Some tasks are assigned to workers, some to machines, and some to external suppliers.
6. The resulting products and services may be exchanged in markets or provided through other institutions.

Later chapters will examine how firms decide which tasks need employees, how much they can afford to pay, and how labour-market institutions and bargaining affect wages.

## 8. Key distinctions to remember

| Term | Meaning | Simple example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Scarcity | Limited resources relative to possible uses | Only four free hours today |
| Poverty | Insufficient resources against a defined basic-needs standard | A household unable to afford adequate food and shelter |
| Shortage | Quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied at the current price | Not enough rental homes available at listed rents |
| Want | Something a person desires | Wanting a new phone |
| Demand | Willingness and ability to buy at a given price | Being ready and able to buy the phone for ₹25,000 |
| Trade-off | What is sacrificed by choosing one option | Study time instead of leisure |
| Opportunity cost | Value of the best alternative forgone | The interview preparation not done because the evening went to a project |

## 9. Evidence notes and limitations

- The definitions of scarcity and opportunity cost are standard introductory economics concepts, supported here by OpenStax's open textbook.
- The examples are teaching illustrations, not empirical claims.
- The distinction between needs and wants varies across social, cultural, and policy contexts. It should not be treated as one universal list.
- The three allocation questions are a teaching framework. Actual economies combine markets, organisations, public policy, households, and informal institutions.

## B. Goods and services: what is being exchanged?

An <b>economic good</b> is something useful or desired that is scarce relative to demand and therefore involves choices about production, allocation, or access. In introductory usage, <b>goods</b> are commonly described as physical, tangible objects, while <b>services</b> are activities or performances that satisfy wants or needs. Examples of goods include rice, clothes, a laptop, and a machine. Examples of services include teaching, medical care, transport, software development, and repair.

The distinction is useful but not absolute in modern economies:

- A physical product often comes bundled with services: a laptop may be sold with installation, warranty, cloud storage, or technical support.
- A digital product such as downloadable software is not a conventional physical object; it can be copied at low marginal cost, but access, licensing, support, infrastructure, and intellectual-property rights still matter.
- Some services create durable outputs or are delivered through physical assets. The key economic question is what need is satisfied and what resources are required, not merely whether something can be touched.

India's NCERT Introductory Microeconomics text explicitly distinguishes tangible goods from intangible services and illustrates how farms, weavers, teachers, and labourers obtain other goods and services through exchange. [NCERT, Introductory Microeconomics, Chapter 1](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/leec201.pdf).

## C. Value is not the same as price

The word <b>value</b> has several meanings. It must not be used as if it had only one precise economic definition.

1. <b>Use value / usefulness:</b> The benefit a person gets from using something. A glass of water is extremely useful to a thirsty person.
2. <b>Subjective value:</b> Different people can value the same item differently because their needs, preferences, circumstances, and alternatives differ.
3. <b>Willingness to pay (WTP):</b> The maximum amount a buyer is willing and able to give up for a unit, given their preferences, income, and alternatives. In demand analysis, the demand curve can be interpreted as willingness to pay for successive units.
4. <b>Market price:</b> The amount actually paid per unit in a transaction or the price at which a market currently trades.
5. <b>Social value:</b> Benefits and costs experienced by society, including effects on people who are not direct buyers or sellers.

A price is an observable exchange term; it is not a complete measurement of usefulness, social value, quality, or human worth.

### Example: a laptop

A student may be willing to pay up to ₹60,000 for a laptop because it supports study and work. A seller may be willing to sell it for ₹50,000. If the agreed transaction price is ₹55,000:

- The buyer pays ₹55,000.
- The buyer's willingness-to-pay ceiling is ₹60,000.
- In the simple model, the buyer receives ₹5,000 of consumer surplus.
- The seller receives the agreed price; the seller's surplus depends on the seller's minimum acceptable price or relevant costs.

These are conceptual amounts, not a claim about the actual market price of any laptop.

OpenStax explains consumer surplus as willingness to pay minus the price actually paid, and producer surplus as the price received minus the minimum amount the producer would have accepted. The demand curve represents willingness to pay across quantities. [OpenStax, Principles of Economics 2e, §3.5](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-2e/pages/3-5-demand-supply-and-efficiency).

<b>Important limitation:</b> WTP is constrained by ability to pay. A person may value a medicine or safe housing greatly but lack the income to express that value as effective market demand. Therefore, market demand is not the same as total human need or social importance.

## D. Why exchange happens

Exchange allows a person or organisation to obtain things they do not produce themselves.

Consider a simple economy:

- A farmer produces rice.
- A weaver produces cloth.
- A teacher provides education.
- A mechanic repairs machines.
- Each specialises in some activity and exchanges part of what they produce or earn for other goods and services.

Without exchange, each household would need to produce many things for itself. Exchange makes specialisation practical.

### Gains from trade

A voluntary exchange can benefit both parties because each expects to receive something they value more than what they give up. This is an <b>ex ante</b> expectation, not a guarantee that every transaction will turn out well. Fraud, coercion, misinformation, unexpected outcomes, and unequal bargaining power can make exchanges harmful or unfair.

The NCERT simple-economy model illustrates this interdependence using a farm, a weaver, a teacher, and a labourer. [NCERT, Chapter 1](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/leec201.pdf).

### Specialisation and the size of the market

Adam Smith's <i>The Wealth of Nations</i> (1776), Book I, Chapter III, argued that the division of labour is limited by the extent of the market. If a specialist cannot find enough customers for a narrow task, specialisation may not be viable. Larger markets allow producers to serve more customers and support more specialised occupations.

Smith's example contrasts isolated villages, where a worker must perform many kinds of work, with larger towns and better-connected trade routes, where specialist occupations can survive. This is a historical mechanism, not a claim that every large market automatically produces fair or efficient outcomes. [Adam Smith, ](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/3300/3300-h/3300-h.htm)[<i>The Wealth of Nations</i>](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/3300/3300-h/3300-h.htm)[, Book I, Chapter III](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/3300/3300-h/3300-h.htm).

<b>Employment connection:</b> A larger market for software, healthcare, logistics, or financial services can support specialised roles that would not be viable if only a few local customers existed. This is one foundation for understanding why global markets can support specialised companies and occupations.

## E. What is a market?

In economics, a <b>market is the set of arrangements through which buyers and sellers interact to exchange a particular good or service</b>. It does not have to be a physical location.

Examples:

- A Vijayawada vegetable market.
- An online marketplace connecting sellers and customers.
- An auction for agricultural produce.
- A market for enterprise software.
- A market for labour, where employers seek workers and workers offer labour services.

A market has participants, an item or service being exchanged, rules and institutions, and some process through which terms of exchange are agreed or determined. Markets may use posted prices, bargaining, auctions, tenders, contracts, or platform-mediated matching.

The NCERT material explains that buyers and sellers need not meet physically; transactions can take place through communication channels and online systems. [NCERT, Chapter 12: Market](https://digital.nios.ac.in/content/214en/Chapter-12.pdf).

## F. Demand, supply, and prices

### Demand

In economics, demand is the relationship between the price of a good or service and the quantity buyers are willing and able to purchase at each price, during a defined period and market.

- <b>Quantity demanded</b> is the amount buyers would purchase at one particular price.
- The <b>law of demand</b> says that, other things equal, a higher price typically reduces quantity demanded and a lower price typically increases it.
- A change in the good's own price causes movement along the demand curve.
- Changes in income, preferences, population, expectations, and prices of substitutes or complements can shift demand.

Demand means more than desire: the buyer must also have the ability to pay. [OpenStax, Principles of Microeconomics 2e, §3.1](https://openstax.org/books/principles-microeconomics-2e/pages/3-1-demand-supply-and-equilibrium-in-markets-for-goods-and-services).

### Supply

Supply is the relationship between price and the quantity producers are willing to offer for sale at each price.

- <b>Quantity supplied</b> is the amount producers offer at one particular price.
- The <b>law of supply</b> says that, other things equal, a higher price typically increases the quantity producers are willing to supply.
- Input costs, technology, taxes, subsidies, regulation, expectations, and natural conditions can shift supply.

The law of supply is a simplified relationship, not a universal prediction for every good at every time. [OpenStax, §3.1](https://openstax.org/books/principles-microeconomics-2e/pages/3-1-demand-supply-and-equilibrium-in-markets-for-goods-and-services).

### Market equilibrium

In the basic competitive supply-and-demand model, the equilibrium price is the price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied.

- If the price is below equilibrium, quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied: a shortage or excess demand.
- If the price is above equilibrium, quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded: a surplus or excess supply.
- Changes in demand or supply can move the equilibrium price and quantity.

OpenStax's illustrative gasoline schedule shows an equilibrium where both quantities equal 600 million gallons at \$1.40 per gallon. Those are textbook example values, not current observed gasoline-market data. [OpenStax, §3.1](https://openstax.org/books/principles-microeconomics-2e/pages/3-1-demand-supply-and-equilibrium-in-markets-for-goods-and-services).

### What prices do

In the basic market model, prices help:

- Communicate relative scarcity.
- Give buyers information about the cost of acquiring another unit.
- Give producers an incentive to expand or reduce supply.
- Coordinate many separate decisions without requiring one central planner to specify every transaction.

But a price is not a perfect information system. It may fail to reflect costs or benefits imposed on third parties; market power, asymmetric information, public goods, externalities, and legal restrictions can affect prices and quantities. [OpenStax, Public Goods](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-2e/pages/13-3-public-goods); [IMF, Public Expenditure and Resource Allocation — Market Failure](https://www.elibrary.imf.org/display/book/9781557752222/ch003.xml).

## G. Public goods: an important boundary case

Not everything socially useful is sold through an ordinary individual transaction.

Economists classify a pure public good by two properties:

- <b>Non-excludable:</b> It is difficult or costly to prevent people from benefiting.
- <b>Non-rival:</b> One person's use does not substantially reduce the amount available to others.

National defence is a standard example. This helps explain why markets alone may not provide the socially desired amount of some goods and why collective financing or public provision may be used. Not every service paid for by government is technically a public good. [OpenStax, Principles of Economics 2e, §13.3](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-2e/pages/13-3-public-goods).

## H. Synthesis: the mechanism connecting value to economic activity

The research supports this simplified chain:

Human wants and needs<br />→ people value different possible solutions differently<br />→ some buyers have willingness and ability to pay<br />→ producers offer goods and services<br />→ buyers and sellers interact through markets or other institutions<br />→ exchange allows specialisation<br />→ market prices and quantities adjust to demand and supply, subject to rules and market imperfections<br />→ producers receive information and incentives about what customers are willing to purchase.

This is a foundation, not a complete explanation of company success. A company must still organise production, cover costs, manage risk, finance investment, and decide whether it can capture enough revenue to continue. Those are later chapters.

## I. Counterarguments, caveats, and common mistakes

1. <b>Price is not the same as value.</b> A high price does not prove high social value; a low price does not prove something is unimportant.
2. <b>Need is not identical to effective demand.</b> Purchasing power determines whether a want becomes market demand.
3. <b>Voluntary does not always mean fair.</b> Information gaps, unequal bargaining power, limited alternatives, and coercion matter.
4. <b>Equilibrium is not justice.</b> A market-clearing price does not guarantee an equitable distribution or access for people without purchasing power.
5. <b>The simple demand-supply model assumes other things equal.</b> Real markets are affected by changing technology, expectations, regulations, market power, and institutions.
6. <b>Not all useful goods are ordinary private goods.</b> Public goods, common resources, and goods with externalities need separate analysis.
7. <b>Specialisation has dependencies and risks.</b> It can raise productivity but can also create reliance on suppliers, infrastructure, transport, and wider markets.

## J. Research conclusion

Goods and services are ways of satisfying wants and needs. People value them differently, and willingness to pay is one market-based expression of that value—but it is constrained by purchasing power and is not a measure of total social importance. Exchange connects people who specialise in different activities. Markets are arrangements that coordinate buyers and sellers; prices emerge through several mechanisms, with supply and demand providing the basic introductory model. Prices can guide production and consumption, but market failures and institutions limit what the simple model can explain.

<b>Why this matters for the book:</b> Companies exist in an economy where customers choose among competing solutions. To understand why companies form, grow, hire, and pay different salaries, we must next understand money, specialisation, and the institutions that make exchange scalable.

# Chapter 1 — Part 3: Money and the Foundations of Exchange

<b>Status:</b> Research record completed retrospectively on 2026-11-13 to formalise the lesson already explained orally.

## Research synthesis

Money is an asset or instrument generally accepted for payment and settlement. In introductory economics it is defined by the functions it performs, not by its physical form.

### The barter coordination problem

Barter is direct exchange of one good or service for another. It can work in small or occasional exchanges, but it becomes difficult when each party must find someone who:

- Has what they want.
- Wants what they have.
- Agrees on the quantity and timing.
- Can assess quality and divisibility.
- Can transport or store the goods until exchange.

This is commonly called the <b>double coincidence of wants</b> problem. The phrase is widely associated with William Stanley Jevons's 1875 work; the broader problem of matching counterparties is older. It is misleading to claim that all societies historically passed through one universal, pure barter stage before money. Historical exchange has also involved credit, obligations, redistribution, commodity money, and social institutions. The lesson is that money reduces the need for direct matching; it is not a complete universal history of how money originated. [Federal Reserve Bank of Richmond, 2024](https://www.richmondfed.org/publications/research/economic_brief/2024/eb_24-01); [IMF, Money: At the Center of Transactions](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/fandd/issues/series/back-to-basics/money).

### Three core functions

1. <b>Medium of exchange:</b> People accept it as payment for goods and services, avoiding the need to barter directly.
2. <b>Unit of account:</b> Prices, debts, and financial records can be expressed in a common unit and compared.
3. <b>Store of value:</b> Purchasing power can be transferred from the present to the future. Money is not a perfect store of value: inflation reduces its purchasing power, and some currencies may lose value rapidly.

Some institutional manuals also identify a fourth function, <b>standard of deferred payment</b>, because money denominates obligations payable in the future. This is often treated as an extension of unit of account and means of settling debts rather than a separate core function in basic teaching. [IMF Monetary and Financial Statistics Manual, Chapter 6](https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/mfsmcg/c6.pdf).

### What makes an instrument useful as money?

Useful characteristics often include acceptability, divisibility, portability, durability, recognisability, standardisation, and relative scarcity/stability. No single physical material is inherently required: historical forms have included commodities, coins, paper notes, and ledger entries.

### Modern money is not only notes and coins

A modern monetary system includes:

- <b>Currency:</b> Notes and coins issued under the monetary authority's framework.
- <b>Commercial bank deposits:</b> Balances that households and firms commonly use to make payments.
- <b>Central bank money:</b> Currency and reserve balances held by eligible institutions at the central bank.

The ECB explains that most money used by the public in the euro area is commercial-bank money, created as banks expand their balance sheets through lending; loan repayment reduces the deposit money created. This is a euro-area institutional explanation, not a direct quantitative claim about India's monetary aggregates. [European Central Bank — What is money?](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/ecb-and-you/explainers/tell-me-more/html/what_is_money.en.html).

### Indian connection: payment rails are not themselves the definition of money

India's payment infrastructure, including NEFT, RTGS, and UPI, enables transfer of money between accounts. A payment system is the mechanism for transferring claims; it should not be confused with the underlying unit of account or with the entire monetary system. RBI's report discusses the three functions of money and the development of India's electronic payment ecosystem. [Reserve Bank of India — Report on Currency and Finance / payment systems discussion](https://rbi.org.in/Scripts/PublicationReportDetails.aspx?ID=1218&UrlPage=).

### Limitations

- Money can lose purchasing power; store of value is a function, not a guarantee.
- Trust, legal institutions, payment infrastructure, and monetary governance affect acceptance.
- The historical story of money is diverse; a simple barter-to-coin-to-paper narrative is only a teaching simplification.
- Different statistical definitions of money (such as narrow and broad aggregates) include different instruments.

# Chapter 1 — Part 4: Capital, Investment, and Risk

<b>Research status:</b> Researched and synthesised<br /><b>Research date:</b> 2026-11-13

## A. Research question

If money makes exchange easier, how do individuals, companies, and governments use resources today to create more productive capacity in the future? What is capital, what is investment, and why does investment involve risk?

## B. Capital has more than one meaning

The word capital is used in different ways. Mixing these meanings creates confusion.

1. <b>Physical capital:</b> Produced assets used to make other goods and services — machines, factory buildings, tools, computers, roads, warehouses, and equipment.
2. <b>Human capital:</b> Skills, knowledge, experience, and capabilities embodied in people that can improve productive activity. A person is not literally a machine or an owned asset; the term refers to productive capabilities.
3. <b>Natural capital:</b> Natural resources and ecosystems that contribute to economic activity and human well-being.
4. <b>Financial capital / funds:</b> Money and financial claims used to finance investment. Money is not identical to a productive machine; finance helps someone acquire or develop productive assets.
5. <b>Intangible produced capital:</b> Assets such as software, databases, research and development, and other intellectual-property products that support future production.

OpenStax distinguishes physical capital, human capital, and technology as complementary drivers of productivity and long-run economic growth. OECD national-accounts classifications also include software, databases, and R&D within intellectual-property products and fixed investment. [OpenStax — Components of Economic Growth](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/20-3-components-of-economic-growth); [OECD — Investment by Asset](https://www.oecd.org/en/data/indicators/investment-by-asset.html).

## C. Investment: giving up resources now for future benefits

In everyday finance, people may call buying shares or gold an investment. In macroeconomics and national accounting, <b>investment</b> has a narrower meaning: adding to produced assets that support future production, along with changes in inventories.

Examples:

- A manufacturing firm purchases a new production line.
- A hospital builds a new facility.
- A software company develops qualifying software and databases.
- A logistics company buys trucks and warehouse equipment.
- A government builds a road or public water system.
- A company adds to inventories of work in progress or finished goods.

The World Bank's national-accounts definition of gross capital formation includes additions to fixed assets and net changes in inventories. [World Bank — Gross Capital Formation metadata](https://databank.worldbank.org/metadataglossary/jobs/series/NE.GDI.TOTL.ZS).

<b>Important distinction:</b> Buying an existing share or bond usually transfers ownership of a financial claim; it does not automatically create a new machine, building, or other produced asset. The funds may support new investment if they finance a new issue or are channelled into productive projects, but secondary-market trading by itself is not the same as gross fixed capital formation.

## D. Gross investment, depreciation, and net investment

Capital assets wear out, become obsolete, or lose productive capacity over time. This is depreciation/consumption of fixed capital.

- <b>Gross investment</b> measures additions before subtracting depreciation.
- <b>Net investment</b> is gross investment minus depreciation.
- If gross investment exceeds depreciation, the productive capital stock tends to grow.
- If gross investment merely replaces worn-out assets, productive capacity may stay roughly constant.
- If gross investment is below depreciation, the capital stock can shrink.

This is a conceptual accounting distinction. Actual national accounts apply detailed asset-specific methods and valuation rules.

## E. Why investment can increase productivity

Imagine two workshops:

- Workshop A has one old machine and produces 10 units per day.
- Workshop B has a newer machine, better tools, and trained operators and produces 30 units per day.

The example is illustrative, not an empirical productivity estimate. More or better physical capital can allow a worker to produce more, improve quality, reduce downtime, or perform tasks that were previously impossible. But equipment alone does not guarantee higher productivity: maintenance, worker skills, management, energy, software, infrastructure, and demand all matter.

OpenStax describes physical capital, human capital, and technology as complementary inputs to growth. A new machine may require training; skilled workers may develop better technology; and technology may only raise output after firms invest in equipment and organisational changes. [OpenStax — Components of Economic Growth](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/20-3-components-of-economic-growth).

## F. Financial capital, savings, and the cost of funds

A company may finance investment through:

- Retained earnings (profits kept in the business).
- Borrowing from banks or bond markets.
- Issuing new shares or attracting equity investors.
- Government grants, subsidies, or public procurement in some cases.
- Venture capital or private equity, depending on the company and stage.

Financing has a cost and constraints. Borrowing requires repayment and interest; equity financing shares ownership and future upside; retained earnings have an opportunity cost because they could be used elsewhere.

Interest rates influence the cost of borrowing and the attractiveness of projects. A project is more likely to be undertaken when its expected benefits justify its costs and risks, but forecasts can be wrong.

## G. Risk, uncertainty, and irreversibility

Investment is forward-looking. A firm commits resources now, hoping for future demand, revenue, productivity, or strategic benefits.

Risks include:

- Customer demand may be lower than expected.
- Technology may become obsolete.
- Construction or implementation may exceed budget.
- Interest rates or exchange rates may change.
- Regulation may change.
- Competitors may launch a better product.
- Supply chains may fail.
- The asset may be difficult to resell or repurpose.

Some investments are partly irreversible: once a specialised factory or platform is built, the company cannot recover all the cost by selling it. This can make firms delay projects when uncertainty is high.

## H. Capital and jobs: the connection

Capital investment changes what work a company needs.

Examples:

- A new factory creates demand for construction, installation, operations, maintenance, quality, logistics, and management.
- Automation equipment may reduce the number of workers needed for some repetitive tasks while increasing demand for technicians, software, controls, and maintenance.
- A software platform investment can increase demand for developers, security engineers, product managers, and cloud operations.
- A new warehouse may create logistics jobs but also change work in nearby distribution networks.

The net effect on total employment is not automatic. It depends on demand growth, substitution of machines for tasks, complementary tasks created, costs, market expansion, and how quickly workers and firms adjust. We will research that mechanism in the later technology and job-creation chapters.

## I. Common confusions and limitations

1. <b>Money is not the same as capital.</b> Money is a means of payment and a financial asset; physical and intangible capital are produced assets used in production.
2. <b>Financial investment is not always real investment.</b> Trading an existing financial asset may transfer ownership without adding productive capacity.
3. <b>Education is not automatically productive human capital.</b> Learning, quality, relevance, health, opportunity to use skills, and institutions affect the economic return.
4. <b>More capital does not guarantee more jobs.</b> Capital can complement labour, substitute for some tasks, or create new demand; the overall employment effect is context-dependent.
5. <b>Investment is risky.</b> Expected returns are not guaranteed, and the future value of an asset may be lower than expected.
6. <b>Capital accumulation is not the only source of growth.</b> Technology, institutions, organisation, skills, and productivity improvements matter too.

## J. Research conclusion

Capital is a set of productive capabilities and assets, not simply a pile of money. Investment uses resources today to create or improve assets that can support future production. Physical capital, human capital, technology, and intangible assets work together; financing makes investment possible but carries costs and risk. Capital investment can create, transform, or displace tasks, so it is one of the mechanisms through which company strategy eventually shapes employment.

# Chapter 1 — Part 5: Productivity and Specialisation

<b>Research status:</b> Researched, synthesised, saved<br /><b>Research date:</b> 2026-11-13<br /><b>Scope:</b> Global concepts, with an India-specific firm-management case<br /><b>Central question:</b> Why can two organisations with the same number of employees produce very different amounts or values of output?

## A. Define productivity correctly

<b>Productivity is a ratio between output and the inputs used to produce it.</b> It is not simply how busy a person looks, how hard someone appears to work, or how many tasks they complete.

A simple expression is:

Productivity = Output / Input

The relevant output and input must be specified. For example:

- Units produced per worker per day.
- Good-quality units produced per labour hour.
- Real value added per hour worked.
- Output relative to a combination of labour, capital, energy, materials, and services.

The OECD defines labour productivity as output per hour worked and emphasises that it reflects how labour is combined with capital, intermediate inputs, technology, and organisational efficiency. It does not measure a worker's personal ability or effort in isolation. [OECD — Labour productivity and utilisation](https://www.oecd.org/en/data/indicators/labour-productivity-and-utilisation.html); [OECD — GDP per hour worked](https://www.oecd.org/en/data/indicators/gdp-per-hour-worked.html).

### Labour productivity and multifactor productivity

1. <b>Labour productivity:</b> Output per worker or, more accurately for many comparisons, output per hour worked.
2. <b>Multifactor productivity (MFP):</b> Output relative to a combined set of inputs, commonly labour and capital, and in some frameworks energy, materials, and purchased services.

Labour productivity can rise because workers have better machines or more capital available, even if the underlying efficiency with which all inputs are combined has not improved. MFP attempts to account for the combined inputs, though measurement is difficult. [Australian Productivity Commission — What is productivity?](https://www.pc.gov.au/what-is-productivity/); [Reserve Bank of Australia — Productivity](https://www.rba.gov.au/education/resources/explainers/productivity.html).

## B. Why the same headcount can produce different output

Imagine two software teams, each with 10 engineers. Team A delivers 4 reliable, customer-valued features per month; Team B delivers 12. This is a hypothetical illustration, not a benchmark.

The difference might come from:

- <b>Skills and experience:</b> Engineers understand the problem and tools better.
- <b>Specialisation:</b> Each person owns a well-understood area rather than constantly switching tasks.
- <b>Technology and capital:</b> Better development tools, infrastructure, automated tests, reusable platforms, and computing resources.
- <b>Management and organisation:</b> Clear priorities, realistic planning, good information flow, and quick decisions.
- <b>Processes and quality:</b> Fewer defects, rework, hand-offs, and production incidents.
- <b>Coordination:</b> Teams share interfaces and standards and avoid duplicated effort.
- <b>Product and task mix:</b> Some work is inherently easier to scale than other work.
- <b>Demand and capacity utilisation:</b> A team with a steady pipeline may use its capacity better than one waiting for approvals or requirements.

A raw output count can mislead. Twelve low-quality features that customers do not use may be less valuable than four reliable features that solve important problems. Productivity comparisons need comparable output quality, scope, and input measurement.

## C. Specialisation and division of labour

<b>Specialisation</b> means concentrating on a narrower set of activities where a person, team, firm, or region develops capability.

<b>Division of labour</b> means breaking a larger production process into distinct tasks and allocating those tasks among different workers or teams.

Adam Smith's famous pin-factory example in <i>The Wealth of Nations</i> (1776), Book I, Chapter I, describes production split into many separate operations. Smith identified three main mechanisms through which division of labour can raise output:

1. Repeated practice increases dexterity and skill at a particular task.
2. Less time is lost switching between unrelated tasks.
3. Repetition and close attention to a task encourage invention or adoption of tools and machinery that simplify work.

This is a foundational historical explanation, not a modern universal estimate of how much every firm gains from specialisation. [Adam Smith, ](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Wealth_of_Nations/Book_I/Chapter_1)[<i>The Wealth of Nations</i>](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Wealth_of_Nations/Book_I/Chapter_1)[, Book I, Chapter I](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Wealth_of_Nations/Book_I/Chapter_1).

### Modern software example

A small software team may have every engineer handle frontend, backend, database, testing, deployment, and support. As the product and team grow, people may specialise:

- Frontend engineering.
- Backend and distributed systems.
- Data engineering.
- Security.
- Site reliability and cloud operations.
- Product design and research.
- Quality engineering.

Specialisation can deepen expertise and reduce task-switching, but it also creates hand-offs and dependencies. A team needs shared interfaces, documentation, coordination, and integration work. Excessive silos can slow delivery.

## D. Specialisation across people, firms, and countries

Specialisation does not happen only inside one company.

- <b>Within a team:</b> Different engineers own different technical domains.
- <b>Between firms:</b> One company makes chips, another designs devices, another provides logistics, another offers cloud infrastructure.
- <b>Across regions and countries:</b> Production stages are located where the relevant capabilities, costs, infrastructure, and market access make them viable.

David Ricardo's comparative-advantage principle explains why parties can gain from specialisation and trade even when one party is more productive in absolute terms in every activity. The key is <b>opportunity cost</b>: specialise in the activity where the relative sacrifice of other output is lower. [OpenStax — Absolute and Comparative Advantage](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-2e/pages/33-1-absolute-and-comparative-advantage).

### Simple numerical illustration

Suppose in one day:

- Person A can produce 10 reports or 5 dashboards.
- Person B can produce 6 reports or 4 dashboards.

A is absolutely more productive in both tasks. But the opportunity costs differ:

- For A, one dashboard costs 2 reports.
- For B, one dashboard costs 1.5 reports.

B has the comparative advantage in dashboards; A has the comparative advantage in reports. If they specialise and trade on mutually beneficial terms, they may produce a larger combined output than if each splits time in the same way. This is a hypothetical teaching example, not observed data.

## E. Scale, learning, and repeatability

Specialisation is easier when there is enough demand to keep a person or team occupied in a narrow role. This links back to Adam Smith's earlier point that the extent of the market limits the division of labour.

Related concepts:

- <b>Learning by doing:</b> Repetition can improve skill and reveal better methods.
- <b>Economies of scale:</b> Average cost can fall as output expands over a relevant range, for example when fixed costs are spread over more units or processes become more efficient.
- <b>Standardisation:</b> Common procedures and interfaces reduce variation and coordination costs.
- <b>Reusable assets:</b> Software libraries, platforms, templates, and automated pipelines allow work to be reused rather than rebuilt.

Scale does not guarantee efficiency. Large organisations may also suffer from bureaucracy, slow decisions, communication overhead, and duplicated systems.

## F. Management is a productivity input — evidence from India

A particularly relevant India-specific study is Bloom, Eifert, Mahajan, McKenzie, and Roberts, <i>Does Management Matter? Evidence from India</i> (World Bank Policy Research Working Paper 5573, 2011; research dated December 2010).

The researchers conducted a field experiment in large Indian textile firms. They randomly assigned plants to receive intensive consulting on modern management practices or serve as controls. The paper reports:

- Average productivity rose by <b>11%</b> in treatment plants.
- Improvements operated through better quality, efficiency, and lower inventory.
- Information systems and data collection increased.
- Decision-making became more decentralised as owners had better information to delegate.
- The experiment covered <b>28 plants across 17 firms</b>, so its result should not be treated as a universal 11% productivity gain for all Indian businesses.

The study is important because it shows that productivity differences are not explained only by worker effort or education. Management routines, information, targets, inventory control, quality systems, and decision rights can matter. [World Bank Working Paper 5573](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/166241468283507372/pdf/WPS5573.pdf).

A broader IMF 2025 India Article IV staff report also discusses productivity differences across Indian firms and sectors, including the role of firm size, resource allocation, competition, R&D, technology adoption, and barriers to firm growth. Its comparisons are specific to the report's data and definitions; they should not be converted into claims about every individual company. [IMF — India 2025 Article IV, Annex IX](https://www.elibrary.imf.org/view/journals/002/2025/314/article-A001-en.xml).

## G. Productivity is not the same as salary

This distinction is essential for the book.

- Productivity is an output-to-input relationship.
- Revenue is money received from sales or other business activity.
- Profit is revenue minus costs under the relevant accounting definition.
- Wages are payments for labour, shaped by labour demand and supply, employer ability and willingness to pay, bargaining power, institutions, and other factors.

A worker can contribute to productivity without personally receiving the full value of that contribution as salary. A high-productivity company may have high wages, but this is not automatic. The relationship between productivity and compensation will be researched in the labour-economics and wage-formation stages.

## H. Limits and counterarguments

1. <b>Quantity is not enough:</b> Output quality, reliability, safety, and customer value must be considered.
2. <b>Labour productivity is not worker effort:</b> It also reflects capital, technology, intermediate inputs, organisation, and scale.
3. <b>Specialisation has coordination costs:</b> More hand-offs and dependencies can offset gains from expertise.
4. <b>Repetitive work can have human costs:</b> Excessive fragmentation may reduce autonomy, variety, learning, and job satisfaction.
5. <b>Bottlenecks matter:</b> Making one stage faster does not raise total output if another stage remains constrained.
6. <b>Scale can create diseconomies:</b> Large firms may accumulate bureaucracy, slow decisions, and communication overhead.
7. <b>Comparisons require care:</b> Different products, quality, prices, working hours, capital intensity, and sector mix make simple output-per-employee comparisons misleading.
8. <b>Productivity is not a moral ranking:</b> It does not measure human worth or the social importance of unpaid and non-market work.

## I. Research conclusion

Two organisations with the same headcount can produce very different output because productivity depends on the full production system, not just the number or effort of employees. Skills, capital, technology, task design, specialisation, management, information, processes, product quality, and coordination all influence how inputs become outputs. Division of labour can raise productivity through learning, reduced switching time, and better tools, but excessive specialisation creates hand-offs and dependencies. Indian field-experimental evidence shows that management practices can materially affect firm productivity, while the measured effect is specific to the studied textile plants.

<b>Connection to the book's main question:</b> Companies with different productivity, technology, organisation, and market positions can generate different amounts of value added and revenue from similar headcounts. This helps explain differences between employers, but it does not by itself explain salary offers. Wage formation requires a separate analysis of labour markets, bargaining, and institutions.

# Stage 1 / Part 6 — Economic Systems and Institutions

<b>Research date:</b> 13 November 2026<br /><b>Status:</b> Researched and synthesised; saved with source-linked canonical JSON. Stage 1 integrated review remains pending.

## 1. Core question

Who decides what an economy produces, how production is organised, who receives the output, and what rules govern those decisions? How do those rules influence firms, investment, innovation, jobs, and wages?

## 2. Economic systems: three ideal types

Economic systems describe how a society coordinates production and distribution. Introductory economics commonly distinguishes three ideal types. These are analytical models, not perfect descriptions of actual countries.

| Ideal type | Main coordination mechanism | Typical ownership / decision pattern | Strengths and risks |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Market-oriented system | Decentralised decisions through prices, exchange, competition and private enterprise | Households and firms make many production and consumption decisions; private ownership is prominent | Can use prices and competition to coordinate dispersed information and reward innovation; can also produce inequality, market power, externalities and underprovided public goods |
| Command / centrally planned system | Administrative direction and plans | Government agencies make major output, input, investment and price decisions; state ownership may be extensive | Can mobilise resources for selected priorities; risks information bottlenecks, weak feedback, shortages, misallocation and weak incentives if targets and accountability are poor |
| Mixed system | Markets combined with public provision, regulation, taxation, transfers and sometimes state-owned enterprises | Private and public actors both make important decisions | Can combine decentralised enterprise with public goods, safeguards and redistribution; outcomes depend on institutional quality, design, state capacity and accountability |

<b>Important correction:</b> “market” does not mean “no government”, and “government involvement” does not automatically mean “command economy”. Even market-oriented economies require laws defining property, contracts, corporate forms, money, liability, competition, taxation and dispute resolution. OpenStax explicitly notes that markets and regulation are entangled and that real economies combine mechanisms. [OpenStax, Principles of Economics 3e, §1.4](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/1-4-how-to-organize-economies-an-overview-of-economic-systems).

## 3. Institutions are the rules of the game

An <b>institution</b> is a durable formal or informal rule, norm, procedure or enforcement arrangement that structures how people interact. An <b>organisation</b> is a group of people deliberately organised to pursue goals within those rules.

Examples:

- Institutions: property law, contract law, court procedures, accounting standards, labour rules, competition law, social norms, monetary arrangements.
- Organisations: a company, court, ministry, trade union, bank, regulator, university or industry association.

A useful distinction: a company is an organisation; the legal rules governing incorporation, limited liability, contracts and insolvency are institutions. A regulator is an organisation; the statute defining its powers, independence and accountability is part of the institutional framework.

Institutions can be formal (written laws and regulations) or informal (trust, conventions, professional norms and business practices). They may be effective or ineffective; the mere existence of a law or agency does not prove that the intended function is being performed.

## 4. What institutions do in an economy

1. <b>Define and protect rights:</b> clarify who owns an asset, who may use it, transfer it, exclude others, or receive income from it.
2. <b>Make promises more credible:</b> contracts and dispute-resolution systems help parties transact without relying only on personal trust.
3. <b>Reduce uncertainty and transaction costs:</b> predictable rules can reduce the time and expense of searching, negotiating, monitoring and enforcing agreements.
4. <b>Coordinate expectations:</b> standards, regulations and stable procedures allow firms, workers, lenders and customers to plan around one another.
5. <b>Limit opportunism:</b> fraud rules, liability, audits and sanctions can discourage cheating, non-payment and unsafe conduct.
6. <b>Enable competition and entry:</b> competition policy and fair market access can limit exclusionary conduct and protect the possibility of new firms entering.
7. <b>Address market failures:</b> public policy may respond to externalities, public goods, information asymmetry and market power.
8. <b>Redistribute and provide social protection:</b> taxes, transfers, education, healthcare and social insurance can affect opportunity, risk and distribution.
9. <b>Support macroeconomic stability:</b> monetary and fiscal frameworks influence inflation, credit conditions, demand, investment and employment.

These functions are not automatic. Rules can be poorly designed, selectively enforced, captured by powerful groups, or administered with insufficient capacity.

## 5. Public goods, externalities and market failure

A <b>public good</b> is generally non-rival (one person's use does not substantially reduce another's) and non-excludable (it is difficult to prevent non-payers from benefiting). Examples often include national defence and some forms of public knowledge. Because users can benefit without paying, private markets may supply less than the socially desired amount.

An <b>externality</b> occurs when an activity imposes costs or benefits on third parties that are not fully reflected in the transaction price. Pollution is a negative externality; vaccination or knowledge spillovers can create positive externalities.

Other possible market failures include information asymmetry, natural monopoly and market power. Government action may improve outcomes through public provision, taxation, subsidies, standards, disclosure rules or competition enforcement—but each intervention has implementation costs and may itself fail.

<b>Do not assume every public service is a pure public good.</b> Education, healthcare and transport have mixed characteristics; access can often be restricted and capacity can be congested. They are frequently discussed as merit goods or services with large social spillovers, not necessarily as textbook pure public goods.

## 6. Government failure and institutional capture

Government action is not automatically efficient or neutral. Failure can arise from:

- incomplete information and administrative capacity constraints;
- poorly aligned incentives or weak accountability;
- regulatory capture, where regulated interests shape rules to protect themselves;
- corruption, patronage, exclusion or selective enforcement;
- excessive or contradictory procedures that raise entry and compliance costs;
- policy uncertainty that makes long-term investment harder;
- political incentives favouring short-term visible gains over durable outcomes.

The World Bank's <i>World Development Report 2017: Governance and the Law</i> argues that policy effectiveness depends not only on formal institutional design but also on the political and social setting in which decisions are made. It highlights three functions: <b>commitment, coordination and cooperation</b>. It also analyses how unequal power can produce exclusion, capture and clientelism. Therefore, copying a formal institution from another country does not guarantee the same result; function, enforcement, power relations and local context matter. [World Bank, WDR 2017](https://www.worldbank.org/en/publication/wdr2017).

## 7. Institutions, firms, investment and productivity

The causal pathway is not “good institutions automatically create high salaries”. A more careful mechanism is:

<b>Predictable rights and enforcement → lower uncertainty / more credible agreements → greater willingness to invest and transact → firm entry, expansion and experimentation → potential productivity and employment gains.</b>

The outcome depends on complementary factors such as infrastructure, skills, finance, technology, demand, management and competition.

OECD research using firm- and industry-level data across OECD countries found that product-market regulation and labour-market settings are associated with firm dynamics and productivity; it also emphasised that effects vary by context and technology gap. A separate OECD panel study covering 32 OECD countries over 1985–2013 found associations between more stringent product/labour market regulations and lower investment, while better rule-of-law and legal institutions moderated some negative relationships. These are empirical cross-country associations and model-based findings—not proof that every regulation reduces investment or that deregulation always improves welfare. [OECD, Scarpetta et al. (2002)](https://doi.org/10.1787/547061627526) · [OECD, Égert (2017)](https://doi.org/10.1787/a4ece3c5-en).

The OECD's 2025 synthesis stresses that public institutions support prosperity when they enable fair market access and competition, avoid undue burdens, enforce rules and contracts reliably, and maintain macroeconomic stability. It also notes that corruption weakens investment, competition, entrepreneurship and productivity drivers such as innovation and technology adoption. [OECD, Government at a Glance 2025, “Institutions and growth”](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/2025/06/government-at-a-glance-2025_70e14c6c/full-report/institutions-and-growth_3a01ad72.html).

## 8. India: a mixed economy with a changing policy framework

India's economic system should not be reduced to a one-word label. It combines private enterprise and markets with public-sector enterprises, public infrastructure and services, regulation, taxation, welfare programmes and constitutional commitments to social and economic justice.

- <b>Post-independence framework:</b> India adopted planning and a substantial public-sector role, especially in strategic and basic industries, while private enterprise continued to operate within extensive licensing and controls.
- <b>1991 reforms:</b> A balance-of-payments crisis and wider macroeconomic imbalances preceded reforms that liberalised trade and investment, simplified firm entry in many areas, reduced industrial licensing and expanded private-sector participation. The Government's Economic Survey describes the reforms as a shift toward greater market forces while retaining a mixed economy.
- <b>Later evolution:</b> Reform proceeded gradually and unevenly. The Economic Survey account identifies telecom reform and private participation, with a strengthened regulatory framework including TRAI, as important developments. Public enterprises and state capacity remained part of the system.
- <b>Constitutional context:</b> Directive Principles include promoting welfare and social, economic and political justice; minimising inequalities; securing adequate livelihood; distributing material resources to serve the common good; preventing harmful concentration of wealth and productive resources; and protecting workers. These principles guide governance but are not directly enforceable by courts in the same way as fundamental rights (Article 37).

Sources: [Government of India, Economic Survey chapter on product and capital market reforms](https://www.indiabudget.gov.in/budget2024-25%28I%29/economicsurvey/doc/echapter.pdf); [Constitution of India, Articles 37–39](https://www.legislative.gov.in/static/uploads/2025/07/359f70a69695affb9d72f8393102bd2e.pdf); [Government of India, 1991 industrial policy reform archive](https://www.indiabudget.gov.in/budget_archive/es1991-92_A/2%20The%20Payments%20Crisis.pdf).

<b>What this means for employment:</b> Indian jobs are shaped by both market demand and public rules. Telecom licensing and regulation, public infrastructure investment, education policy, labour regulation, tax policy, trade policy, public procurement and financial regulation can change which firms enter, where they invest, what skills they demand and how work is organised. The 1991-era opening of sectors and later telecom reforms are part of the historical context for the growth of private services and IT-related activity; they are not a single-cause explanation of India's IT industry.

## 9. Comparing countries without stereotypes

A useful comparison asks specific questions rather than assigning simplistic labels:

- Who owns firms and productive assets?
- How are prices and investment decisions made?
- How much is produced or financed publicly?
- How predictable are laws, contracts and administrative decisions?
- How contestable are markets and how easy is firm entry?
- What protections and services are provided to workers and households?
- How capable and accountable are public institutions?

The United States is strongly market-oriented but has extensive public regulation, taxation, social programmes and public spending. Nordic economies combine private firms and open markets with larger tax-funded welfare states and coordinated labour institutions. China combines extensive market exchange and private enterprise with strong state ownership and direction in strategic sectors and significant party-state influence. These are broad descriptions; sectoral arrangements and outcomes vary. Avoid treating any country as a pure type or assuming that one institutional feature alone explains its growth.

## 10. Implications for jobs and salary differences

Institutions influence the environment in which jobs are created, but they do not set each salary mechanically.

- Contract enforcement and predictable business rules can support outsourcing, supplier relationships, startups and investment.
- Competition and entry rules can affect whether new employers emerge and whether workers have alternative employers.
- Education and training institutions shape the supply and quality of skills.
- Labour laws, collective bargaining, minimum-wage rules and social insurance influence employment conditions and bargaining.
- Taxation, public procurement and infrastructure investment can shift demand across industries and regions.
- Corruption, exclusion and regulatory capture can protect incumbents, restrict opportunity and distort resource allocation.

A graduate's pay still depends on the role, skills, productivity, scarcity, employer economics, labour supply, bargaining power, geography, selection and compensation structure. Institutions are part of the causal system, not a complete explanation.

## 11. Research limitations and counterarguments

1. Market and command are ideal types; real economies are hybrids and change over time.
2. A law on paper does not establish effective enforcement or equal access.
3. Institutions may be both a cause and an outcome of economic development; causality is difficult to isolate.
4. Cross-country comparisons face measurement, endogeneity and context problems.
5. Regulation can correct market failures and protect workers; poorly designed regulation can also impose costs or protect incumbents.
6. Deregulation can increase competition in some settings but may worsen externalities, market power or worker insecurity in others.
7. Government capacity and political incentives matter as much as formal institutional design.
8. There is no single institutional blueprint that can be transplanted unchanged across countries.

## 13. Part 6 conclusion

An economy is not simply “the market” or “the government”. It is a set of markets, organisations, laws, informal norms and public institutions. Markets coordinate many decisions through prices, while institutions establish the rights and rules that make exchange possible and help address problems markets do not solve well. Public intervention can improve outcomes, but it can also fail through weak capacity, poor incentives or capture. For employment, these arrangements influence firm entry, investment, competition, skill formation and bargaining—thereby shaping the number and quality of jobs—without directly dictating an individual's salary.

# Stage 1 / Part 7 — Economic Growth, Development and Structural Transformation

<b>Research date:</b> 13 November 2026<br /><b>Status:</b> Researched and synthesised; canonical JSON saved. Stage 1 integrated review remains pending.

## 1. The central distinction

<b>Economic growth</b> is an increase in the quantity of goods and services an economy produces over time, usually measured using the growth rate of real GDP or real GVA. <b>Economic development</b> is broader: it concerns improvements in people's capabilities and living conditions, including health, education, material security, opportunity, institutions and the distribution and sustainability of progress.

Growth can create resources that make development easier, but growth alone does not guarantee that benefits are widely shared, that health and education improve, or that environmental costs are controlled.

## 2. How to measure growth carefully

- <b>GDP at current prices (nominal GDP):</b> values final domestic production using prices of the period. It changes because of both quantities and prices.
- <b>Real GDP:</b> adjusts for price changes using the national-accounts methodology, making it more useful for tracking changes in production volume over time.
- <b>GDP per capita:</b> GDP divided by population. It is an average, not the income of a typical person and not a measure of distribution.
- <b>GVA:</b> measures value added by producers; GDP at market prices is related to total GVA plus product taxes minus product subsidies.
- <b>Labour productivity:</b> output per worker or, preferably when hours are available, output per hour. It helps explain how output can rise without proportional increases in employment.
- <b>Human Development Index (HDI):</b> combines indicators for health, education and standard of living. It complements, rather than replaces, GDP measures.

GDP is not a complete welfare measure. It does not directly reveal who receives income, unpaid household production, leisure, environmental depletion, pollution, insecurity or quality of public services. Use real GDP growth to discuss production; use per-capita, distributional, labour-market, health, education and environmental indicators to discuss development. [IMF, “Gross Domestic Product: An Economy’s All”](https://www.imf.org/en/Publications/fandd/issues/Series/Back-to-Basics/gross-domestic-product-gdp).

## 3. Why economies grow: main channels

1. <b>More labour input:</b> population growth, higher labour-force participation, migration or more hours worked can increase total output, though output per person need not rise.
2. <b>More physical capital:</b> machinery, buildings, infrastructure, electricity, logistics and digital networks can raise productive capacity.
3. <b>Human capital:</b> education, health, training and experience can improve workers' capabilities.
4. <b>Technology and innovation:</b> new products, processes, software, scientific knowledge and better production methods can raise output from existing inputs.
5. <b>Better allocation:</b> moving workers and capital toward more productive firms, occupations, regions and sectors can increase aggregate productivity.
6. <b>Institutions and organisation:</b> predictable rules, competition, management quality, finance and public capacity can support investment and adoption of productive methods.
7. <b>Demand and utilisation:</b> firms may produce more when demand recovers and idle capacity is used; this cyclical increase is different from a permanent rise in productive capacity.

A useful accounting distinction is between <b>factor accumulation</b> (more labour and capital) and <b>productivity growth</b> (more output from given inputs). Growth accounting decomposes observed growth into input contributions and a residual often called total factor productivity (TFP). TFP is not a direct measurement of “technology alone”; it can also reflect measurement error, utilisation, omitted inputs, quality changes and institutional or organisational effects.

## 4. Structural transformation: the economy changes shape

<b>Structural transformation</b> is the long-run reallocation of economic activity and resources across sectors, firms, occupations and locations as an economy develops. A common historical pattern is:

- declining shares of employment and output in agriculture;
- industrialisation and, in many historical cases, a rising manufacturing share during an intermediate phase;
- expanding services, including modern tradable services and domestic services;
- movement of workers toward urban areas and more productive firms and activities.

This is a stylised pattern, not a universal law. Some countries have experienced services-led transitions without a large manufacturing employment boom. Manufacturing's share may peak and later decline as productivity rises and consumer demand shifts. Services are highly heterogeneous: software, finance and professional services differ substantially from low-productivity informal trade or personal services.

Structural transformation can raise aggregate productivity when labour and capital move from lower- to higher-productivity activities. But it can also fail to deliver broad gains if workers move into insecure low-productivity urban work, if formal job creation is weak, or if productivity gaps persist. IMF research separates <b>within-sector productivity improvements</b> from <b>reallocation effects</b>; both contribute to aggregate productivity change. [IMF Working Paper 18/64, “Structural Reforms and Labor Reallocation”](https://www.elibrary.imf.org/view/journals/001/2018/064/article-A001-en.xml).

## 5. India's pattern: services-led output, slower employment reallocation

The Indian case is not a simple textbook progression from agriculture to manufacturing to services.

- NITI Aayog's analysis reports that services contributed nearly <b>55% of India's GVA in 2024–25</b>, while primary and secondary sectors contributed approximately 16% and 29%, respectively. GVA shares are not employment shares. [NITI Aayog, ](https://www.niti.gov.in/node/2029)[<i>India’s Services Sector: Insights from GVA Trends and State-Level Dynamics</i>](https://www.niti.gov.in/node/2029).
- The Government of India's Economic Survey 2025–26 reports that services employment increased from <b>22.1% in 1992 to 30.0% in 2022</b>, an 8.9 percentage-point rise. It also reports that agriculture still accounted for approximately <b>46% of employment in 2023–24</b>. These statistics refer to different periods and should not be combined as if they were one same-year sector table. [Economic Survey 2025–26](https://www.indiabudget.gov.in/economicsurvey/doc/echapter.pdf).
- A World Bank report on India's path to high-income status says agriculture accounted for over 45% of employment in 2023–24, manufacturing for around 11%, and modern market services for around 7%; it also argues that India's movement of labour across sectors has contributed less to productivity growth than in some successful East Asian and East European comparators. The report's “modern market services” category is narrower than all services. [World Bank, ](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099022725232041885/pdf/P175027-38446b18-85d1-45c2-8628-9662d283ed24.pdf)[<i>Becoming a High-Income Economy in a Generation</i>](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099022725232041885/pdf/P175027-38446b18-85d1-45c2-8628-9662d283ed24.pdf).
- The IMF's 2025 India Article IV report estimates that, in FY2022/23 and conditional on comparable education levels, output per additional worker in services was more than four times that in agriculture. This is a model-based marginal-product comparison, not a claim that every service worker earns four times every agricultural worker. The report identifies land rights, migrant social protection, transport infrastructure and limited job creation as constraints on reallocation. [IMF, India: 2025 Article IV Consultation](https://www.elibrary.imf.org/view/journals/002/2025/314/article-A001-en.xml).

<b>Interpretation:</b> India has a large services contribution to output, but a much smaller share of workers in services than the GVA share might suggest. Agriculture continues to employ a large workforce. The challenge is not simply “grow services”; it is to expand productive, accessible employment across manufacturing and diverse services, raise productivity within agriculture, improve worker mobility and ensure that workers can move into better opportunities.

## 6. Why structural change matters for jobs and salaries

- New sectors create demand for different tasks and skills; old sectors may shrink, reorganise or become more capital-intensive.
- Productivity growth can raise wages when workers have bargaining power and labour markets transmit productivity gains, but this is not automatic.
- High-output sectors may employ relatively few workers; high employment shares do not necessarily mean high productivity or high wages.
- Urbanisation can improve access to firms, infrastructure and specialised labour markets, but housing, transport and migration barriers can prevent workers from benefiting.
- Education and training matter, but skills alone cannot create enough good jobs if firms, investment and demand are absent.
- Labour reallocation can increase aggregate output while imposing transition costs on displaced workers and regions. Social protection, portability of benefits and retraining can influence who bears those costs.

This connects directly to later stages on firm economics, labour demand, skills, hiring, wage formation and India's labour market.

## 7. Development beyond output

A fuller development assessment combines:

- income and consumption, including distribution and poverty;
- health, nutrition and life expectancy;
- education, learning and skills;
- employment quantity, job quality, security and participation;
- gender and regional opportunity;
- infrastructure and access to essential services;
- environmental sustainability, resilience and exposure to climate risks;
- institutions, rights and people's ability to make meaningful choices.

No single index captures every dimension. HDI is a compact comparison tool, but it does not fully measure inequality, poverty, empowerment, security or environmental sustainability. Use multiple indicators and state their scope.

## 8. Limits and counterarguments

1. GDP growth can coexist with rising inequality, weak job creation or environmental degradation.
2. Per-capita GDP is an average and can conceal large differences among households and regions.
3. Sector categories hide substantial differences between high- and low-productivity firms within the same sector.
4. Manufacturing-led transformation has been historically important, but the appropriate development path varies with technology, trade, demographics, geography and capabilities.
5. Services-led growth can be productive and exportable, but some services are difficult to scale or trade internationally and may not absorb large numbers of less-skilled workers.
6. Reallocation can be disruptive; movement into a nominally higher-productivity sector does not guarantee secure or better work for every individual.
7. TFP is a residual, not a clean causal measure of technology.
8. Cross-country comparisons depend on definitions, survey coverage, national-account revisions and reference years.

# Stage 1 — Part 8: Trade, Globalisation and International Economic Integration

<b>Research date:</b> 13 November 2026<br /><b>Status:</b> Evidence reviewed; canonical research record appended below. Stage 1 integrated review remains pending.

## 1. The central idea

Trade means buying and selling goods or services across borders. Globalisation is broader: it includes trade, investment, finance, technology, data, migration and cross-border production networks. A modern product may be designed in one country, use components from several others, be assembled elsewhere, and be sold worldwide.

Trade matters for employment because it changes <b>what firms produce, where tasks are performed, which inputs firms can buy, how large their markets become, and which workers they need</b>. It can create jobs and destroy or transform jobs at the same time.

## 2. Why countries and firms trade

The key idea is <b>comparative advantage</b>: specialise where the opportunity cost is relatively lower, then trade for other goods and services. This can create gains even when one country is more productive in every activity. Absolute advantage asks who can produce more with the same inputs; comparative advantage asks what each gives up to produce one more unit.

Trade can also arise from economies of scale, product variety, differences in resources and skills, access to technology, seasonal availability, and the need for specialised intermediate inputs. It is not only finished goods crossing borders: components, cloud services, software, consulting, finance, design and logistics are traded too.

## 3. Global value chains: production across borders

A global value chain (GVC) divides a product's activities across locations: research and design, components, processing, assembly, logistics, marketing, software and after-sales service. A country can enter a chain by importing components, adding labour or processing, and exporting the assembled product. This is <b>backward participation</b>. Supplying domestic inputs that are used in another country's exports is <b>forward participation</b>.

Imported content is not automatically a weakness. If imported components help a domestic factory achieve scale, quality and export competitiveness, absolute domestic value added and employment may rise even if the domestic-value-added share per unit initially looks modest. The policy question is whether supplier capabilities, skills, technology and domestic linkages deepen over time.

## 4. How trade creates, changes and displaces jobs

- <b>Direct:</b> export factories, software exporters, consulting firms and other exporting businesses hire workers.
- <b>Indirect:</b> suppliers, packaging, transport, ports, warehousing, maintenance, finance, testing and professional services gain demand.
- <b>Productivity and scale:</b> access to larger markets and better inputs can increase output, investment and demand for specialised tasks.
- <b>Consumer and downstream gains:</b> cheaper or better imported goods can raise purchasing power and lower costs for domestic producers.
- <b>Displacement:</b> firms exposed to import competition may shrink or close; tasks may move abroad; workers in concentrated regions can face long transitions.
- <b>Task transformation:</b> globally competitive firms may adopt new standards, software, automation and quality systems, changing the skills required rather than simply increasing headcount.

The net national gain does not mean every worker gains. New jobs may appear in different firms, sectors, cities or skill categories from the jobs that disappear. Retraining, mobility, social protection, competition and regional investment affect whether displaced workers can access the new opportunities.

## 5. Evidence: global and India

### Global trade and jobs

- WTO's <i>World Trade Statistics 2025</i> reports world trade in goods and commercial services of <b>US\$34.65 trillion in 2025</b>, up <b>7%</b>; goods trade grew <b>6%</b>, services <b>8%</b>, and services represented <b>27.6%</b> of global trade. [WTO — World Trade Statistics 2025](https://www.wto.org/english/res_e/statis_e/world_trade_statistics_e.htm)
- The World Bank's cross-country work covering <b>1995–2019</b> reports that a <b>10% increase in exports was associated with</b> employment levels higher by <b>3.1%</b>, labour earnings by <b>3.9%</b>, and productivity by <b>0.95%</b> in its sample. These are reported cross-country relationships, not guaranteed causal effects or India-specific forecasts. The links were weaker in low-income economies and diminished after the 2007 global financial crisis. [World Bank — Leveraging Trade for More and Better Jobs](https://www.worldbank.org/en/topic/trade/publication/leveraging-trade-for-more-and-better-jobs)
- WTO estimates that jobs embodied in gross exports averaged <b>28% of employment in 2019</b> across <b>61 economies</b> with available data, up by over 20% relative to 1995. This includes upstream domestic supplier jobs, not only employees directly working for exporters. [WTO — World Trade Report 2024](https://www.wto.org/english/res_e/booksp_e/wtr24_e/wtr24_e.pdf)

### India: export integration

- India's share of global merchandise exports rose from <b>1.0% (2005)</b> to <b>1.8% (2024)</b>. Its share of global commercial services exports rose from <b>2.0%</b> to <b>4.3%</b> over the same period.
- In <b>FY2024–25</b>, total exports of goods and services were <b>US\$825.3 billion</b>, imports were <b>US\$919.9 billion</b>, and the combined trade deficit was <b>US\$94.7 billion</b>. The services trade surplus cushioned the merchandise trade deficit. A trade deficit is not, by itself, a jobs score: imports can include productive machinery and intermediate inputs as well as consumer goods.
- India's backward GVC participation ratio (foreign value added as a share of gross exports) rose from <b>10.7% in 1995</b> to <b>25.5% in 2012</b>, then moderated to <b>17.2% in 2020</b>. Vietnam's corresponding ratio was close to <b>48% in 2020</b>. The comparison points to potential for deeper integration; it does not prove that copying Vietnam's policy mix would automatically reproduce its outcomes.
- The Economic Survey argues that imported intermediate inputs can help labour-rich economies enter export production, scale up and increase absolute domestic value added and employment over time—provided input tariffs, logistics, customs, regulation and investment conditions are competitive.

Sources: [Economic Survey 2025–26, Chapter 4](https://www.indiabudget.gov.in/economicsurvey/doc/echapter.pdf) and [Chapter 8](https://www.indiabudget.gov.in/economicsurvey/doc/echapter.pdf).

## 6. What can go wrong, and who captures the gains?

Trade gains are not automatically shared equally. More productive firms and regions may capture export opportunities; workers in import-competing industries may lose jobs or bargaining power. Small suppliers can be dependent on large lead firms and may have limited ability to negotiate prices. Consumers may gain from lower prices even when some workers lose income. Regional concentration and barriers to moving, retraining, obtaining finance or accessing export markets can make adjustment especially difficult.

WTO's 2024 review stresses that trade can raise aggregate welfare while some workers and regions suffer losses. OECD's 2026 analysis of 14 OECD countries likewise finds that local exposure to import competition can create persistent manufacturing job losses and social costs even when employment gains appear elsewhere over time. These are not contradictory: national averages can hide concentrated local disruption. [WTO World Trade Report 2024](https://www.wto.org/english/res_e/booksp_e/wtr24_e/wtr24_e.pdf) · [OECD Employment Outlook 2026](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-employment-outlook-2026_7e710f54-en/full-report/component-7.html)

## 7. Trade policy: the practical trade-offs

Tariffs can protect a domestic activity or raise government revenue, but they also increase costs for importers, consumers and downstream exporters that use imported inputs. Quotas and non-tariff measures can address legitimate safety or quality concerns, but opaque or excessive requirements can become barriers. Trade agreements can improve market access and predictability, but firms need the capacity, standards, logistics and finance to use that access. Policy should therefore assess both upstream producers and downstream users, not treat every import as harmful or every export as automatically beneficial.

## 8. Implications for jobs and compensation

Trade expands the market available to firms and can raise demand for workers in export-oriented and supplier industries. It can also increase competition and change the tasks firms perform. Compensation may rise where export demand meets scarce skills and productive firms, but it is mediated by productivity, labour supply, employer bargaining power, firm rents, institutions and geography. Export revenue is not a direct salary formula.

For Indian engineering graduates, global integration creates work in IT and software services, SaaS, cloud, cybersecurity, data engineering, consulting, fintech, semiconductor software, manufacturing automation, quality systems and supply-chain technology. It widens the opportunity set, but it does not guarantee fresher vacancies or a particular salary.

## 9. Evidence limitations

The WTO, World Bank and Indian Economic Survey figures use different geographies, time periods, definitions and denominators. Gross export value is not domestic value added. Employment embodied in exports includes indirect supplier employment. Cross-country associations must not be converted into a causal forecast for India or an individual worker. GVC participation ratios measure imported value added in exports, not job quality. The research therefore keeps each statistic attached to its own source, period and definition.

## 10. Part 8 conclusion

International trade connects countries and firms through goods, services and global value chains. Comparative advantage and specialisation can raise productivity, variety and aggregate income; imported inputs can also help domestic exporters scale. But gains are uneven: consumers and expanding firms may benefit while import-competing workers and regions face concentrated adjustment costs. India's growing services-export share is a strength, while deeper manufacturing and GVC integration could widen supplier and employment linkages if supported by competitive inputs, logistics, skills, finance and predictable institutions. Trade creates opportunities and reallocates work; it does not mechanically determine jobs or salaries.

## 1. Integrated causal model

The eight parts now form one connected economic foundation:

1. <b>Scarcity and choice:</b> time, labour, capital, land, knowledge and attention are limited relative to competing uses. Choice creates trade-offs and opportunity costs.
2. <b>Value, goods, services and markets:</b> people and firms produce offerings; exchange connects buyers and sellers; prices communicate information and incentives under particular market conditions.
3. <b>Money:</b> money reduces the frictions of barter and supports exchange, accounting and saving. Modern payment rails are not identical to money itself.
4. <b>Capital, investment and risk:</b> savings and finance can fund productive assets and capabilities; investment is forward-looking and uncertain.
5. <b>Productivity and specialisation:</b> output per unit of input can rise through skills, tools, technology, division of labour, management and coordination. Comparative advantage explains potential gains from specialisation.
6. <b>Institutions and economic systems:</b> property rights, contracts, competition, regulation, public goods and state capacity shape incentives and the costs/risks of economic activity.
7. <b>Growth, development and structural transformation:</b> output can expand through input accumulation, productivity and reallocation; development also includes health, education, opportunity, security and distribution.
8. <b>Trade and globalisation:</b> cross-border specialisation, services trade, investment and value chains enlarge markets and change where tasks are performed, while distributing gains and adjustment costs unevenly.

<b>Integrated chain:</b> scarcity → choices and opportunity cost → production and exchange → prices and markets → money and finance → capital formation → productivity and specialisation → institutions and incentives → growth and structural change → international integration. The chain is not one-way: trade, technology, institutions, competition and macroeconomic conditions feed back into firms' choices, investment and work organisation.

## 2. What the eight parts collectively establish

- Economic activity is organised around choices under scarcity, not simply around money or jobs.
- Markets and prices coordinate many decisions, but do not guarantee fairness, complete information or efficient outcomes in every situation.
- Money enables exchange at scale; capital and investment expand productive capacity but involve uncertainty and financing constraints.
- Productivity is about output relative to inputs. It is not a measure of human worth and does not mechanically determine an individual's wage.
- Institutions shape the rules and predictability within which households, firms and governments act. Both market failures and government failures matter.
- GDP growth, development and structural transformation are related but distinct. A growing economy can still have weak job creation, inequality or regional disruption.
- Trade can create direct and indirect jobs, lower input costs and widen markets, while also displacing workers and firms exposed to competition.
- Across all parts, salary must not be explained as a fixed percentage of revenue, GDP, exports or individual output. Wage formation also requires labour demand and supply, firm rents, bargaining, institutions, market power, skills and geography—covered in later stages.

# Stage 2 — Theory of the Firm

## Part 1: Why Firms Exist — Markets, Coordination and Transaction Costs

<b>Research date:</b> 13 November 2026<br /><b>Research status:</b> Foundational research completed for Part 1; Stage 2 remains in progress.

## 1. The central question

If people and businesses can buy and sell through markets, why do firms exist at all? Why doesn't every worker act as an independent seller, and why doesn't one giant company produce everything?

This is the starting question of the theory of the firm. It connects the economic foundations in Stage 1 to the organisations that actually hire workers.

## 2. What is a firm?

A firm is an organised economic entity that coordinates some productive activities through internal decision rights, authority, routines, teams and continuing relationships. It does not arrange every task as a separate one-time market purchase.

A market coordinates separate parties through prices, voluntary exchange and contracts. A hierarchy coordinates activities inside an organisation through managerial direction, reporting, budgets, internal rules and decision rights.

A firm is not simply a building, a brand or a legal registration. Those may be visible parts of a business, but the economic question is how productive activities are coordinated and controlled.

## 3. Coase's question: why not use the market for everything?

In <i>The Nature of the Firm</i> (1937), economist Ronald Coase asked why firms exist in an economy where prices already coordinate exchange.

His answer begins with <b>the cost of using the price mechanism</b>. A business using the market may have to discover relevant prices and suppliers, compare quality, negotiate terms, draft and enforce contracts, monitor performance, coordinate delivery, adapt agreements and resolve disputes. These are transaction costs, additional to the direct production cost.

When a firm organises work internally, it may avoid repeatedly negotiating a separate contract for every small task. A manager can assign work, coordinate a team and adjust priorities within an ongoing relationship. This is one reason internal organisation can be cheaper than repeated market contracting.

Coase's 1991 Nobel Prize lecture restated the idea: markets and firms are alternative ways of coordinating production, and both have costs. An efficient economy uses a mixture rather than relying exclusively on one.

Sources: [Coase, The Nature of the Firm (1937)](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/j.1468-0335.1937.tb00002.x) · [Coase, Nobel Prize Lecture (1991)](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/1991/coase/lecture/)

## 4. Why doesn't one company do everything?

Internal organisation is not free either. As a firm grows, it can face more management layers, slower decisions, coordination problems across teams and locations, information overload, managerial mistakes, weaker incentives, bureaucracy and duplicated work.

Coase's boundary idea is comparative: a firm tends to organise an additional transaction internally while the cost of doing so is lower than the cost of using the market or another organisation. Its expansion is limited when internal coordination becomes more expensive or less effective.

This is a conceptual economic rule, not a universal formula that can be measured exactly in every company.

## 5. Williamson: transaction costs depend on the type of transaction

Oliver Williamson developed transaction-cost economics beyond the broad Coase insight. His 1979 paper identifies three important dimensions:

1. <b>Uncertainty:</b> How difficult is it to predict future conditions and specify every possible contingency?
2. <b>Frequency:</b> Is the transaction occasional or repeated over a long period?
3. <b>Asset specificity:</b> Does one party need to make a specialised investment that has much less value outside this particular relationship?

A supplier that buys a highly specialised machine for one customer may become dependent on that customer. If the customer later demands a lower price, the supplier may struggle to find another buyer for the machine. This is a potential <b>hold-up problem</b>.

The parties might respond through a long-term contract, a relational agreement, a partnership, shared ownership or vertical integration. The best arrangement depends on the transaction.

Williamson therefore compares three broad governance modes:

- <b>Market:</b> independent parties transact through prices and contracts.
- <b>Hybrid:</b> long-term contracts, alliances, partnerships and other arrangements preserve separate ownership but add coordination safeguards.
- <b>Hierarchy:</b> activities are brought inside one organisation under common ownership or managerial authority.

Source: [Williamson, Transaction-Cost Economics (1979)](https://www.edegan.com/pdfs/Williamson%20%281979%29%20-%20Transaction%20Cost%20Economics.pdf)

## 6. Ownership, control and incomplete contracts

Not every future event can be written into a contract. Contracts are therefore often incomplete. Oliver Hart's property-rights approach highlights <b>residual control rights</b>: who has authority to decide how an asset is used when the contract does not specify what should happen.

Ownership can affect bargaining power and incentives to invest. If one party must make an important relationship-specific investment, the allocation of control over the relevant asset may influence whether that investment is worthwhile.

Source: [Hart, Firms, Contracts, and Financial Structure (1995)](https://academic.oup.com/book/3631)

## 7. Firms also have internal governance costs

Bringing work inside a company may reduce repeated market negotiation, but it does not eliminate conflicts of interest.

Jensen and Meckling's 1976 theory examines <b>agency costs</b> that can arise when ownership and control are separated. Owners or investors may delegate decisions to managers. Managers may have different incentives, information or objectives. Monitoring, incentive systems, bonding arrangements and residual losses are part of the resulting agency-cost problem.

This is a separate but complementary lens: Coase helps explain why activities are organised inside firms rather than through markets; agency theory helps explain some of the problems created inside firms.

Source: [Jensen and Meckling, Theory of the Firm (1976)](<https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-405X(76)90026-X>)

## 8. A practical example: an AI company deciding what to build internally

Imagine an AI startup building a business application.

| Activity | Possible arrangement | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Core product architecture | Internal engineering team | Continuous work, firm-specific knowledge and tight coordination |
| Commodity cloud hosting | External cloud provider | Specialist scale and standardised service may lower total cost |
| One-time logo design | Independent designer or agency | Occasional work with many available specialists |
| Security testing | Hybrid | Internal ownership of security plus independent external testing |
| Sensitive model/data pipeline | Often internal or tightly governed | Confidentiality, reliability, control and specialised knowledge |

There is no universal answer. The decision depends on total cost, quality, speed, reliability, switching costs, data and intellectual-property risk, supplier alternatives and strategic importance—not merely on whether an employee's salary is lower than a supplier's invoice.

## 9. Why this matters for jobs

A firm creates an internal employment relationship when it repeatedly coordinates a worker's tasks through teams, schedules, shared resources, management and ongoing direction rather than buying every output as a separate market transaction.

This helps explain why jobs exist inside organisations. But it does not mean every task should be an employee job. Firms also use contractors, freelancers, agencies, suppliers, consultants and platform-mediated work.

The next parts will examine the employment relationship, job design and make-or-buy decisions in more detail. Wage formation is addressed later in labour economics; firm theory alone does not determine an employee's salary.

## 10. Important limitations

- Transaction costs can become a vague explanation unless the costs and transaction characteristics are specified.
- Firms also arise and grow because of production scale economies, finance, innovation, learning, regulation, taxes, market power and strategic control.
- Markets are not always competitive; hierarchies are not automatically efficient or fair.
- Long-term contracts, alliances, licensing, franchises and digital platforms blur a simple market-versus-firm distinction.
- Coase, Williamson, agency theory and incomplete-contract theory are complementary analytical lenses, not one universal law.

## 11. Part 1 conclusion

Firms exist partly because coordinating every productive activity through separate market contracts can be costly. Internal organisation can reduce search, negotiation and adaptation costs, but it creates its own management, coordination and agency costs. Firm boundaries emerge from comparing markets, hybrid arrangements and hierarchy for particular transactions.

<b>Simple takeaway:</b> companies hire and organise people when doing the work together inside the organisation is more effective than buying every task separately. But they outsource when an external specialist or market arrangement is better.

# Stage 2 — Theory of the Firm

## Part 2: Transaction Costs, Firm Boundaries and Make-or-Buy Decisions

<b>Research date:</b> 13 November 2026<br /><b>Research status:</b> Research completed and canonical record prepared.

## 1. The central question

Part 1 explained why firms exist. Part 2 asks a practical question: for a particular activity, should a company make it internally, buy it from an outside supplier, or form an alliance?

This is the <b>make-or-buy decision</b>. It is made activity by activity, not necessarily once for the entire company.

## 2. Three choices, not just two

| Choice | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <b>Make</b> | Perform the activity inside the firm | Hire engineers to build the core product |
| <b>Buy</b> | Purchase from an external supplier | Buy standard cloud hosting |
| <b>Ally</b> | Coordinate through a continuing relationship while keeping separate ownership | Long-term cloud or specialist-services partnership |

Long-term contracts, joint ventures, alliances, licensing, franchises and relational supplier arrangements sit between one-time market purchases and full ownership. Geyskens, Steenkamp and Kumar's 2006 meta-analysis explicitly studied make, buy and ally choices. [Study](https://doi.org/10.5465/AMJ.2006.21794670)

## 3. The biggest mistake: comparing salary with an invoice

Comparing only employee salaries with a vendor quotation is incomplete. A proper decision compares <b>total lifecycle cost</b>.

<b>Making internally may include:</b>

- Employee compensation, benefits and management time.
- Recruitment, onboarding and training.
- Equipment, software, facilities and capital.
- Coordination, internal bureaucracy and performance evaluation.
- Capacity utilisation and opportunity cost.

<b>Buying externally may include:</b>

- Supplier price and margin.
- Searching for suppliers and checking capability.
- Negotiation, legal work and contract design.
- Integration, quality checks, monitoring and audits.
- Renegotiation, disputes and enforcement.
- Switching, migration, exit and transition costs.
- Supplier failure, lock-in, data/IP exposure and disruption risk.

<b>An alliance may include:</b>

- Contract and governance design.
- Joint planning and relationship management.
- Shared investments and dependence.
- Coordination, dispute resolution, risk sharing and exit costs.

<b>Decision principle:</b> choose the feasible arrangement with the best risk-adjusted total value over the relevant time horizon—not simply the lowest quoted price. Costs must be compared at the same scope, quality, volume, time horizon and risk assumptions.

## 4. Transaction characteristics that change the decision

### A. Asset specificity

Asset specificity means an investment has much less value outside the relationship for which it was made.

- <b>Site specificity:</b> a facility is located next to one particular customer or resource.
- <b>Physical specificity:</b> specialised machinery, tooling or infrastructure is designed for one buyer.
- <b>Human specificity:</b> workers learn customer-specific processes, systems or knowledge.
- <b>Dedicated assets:</b> capacity is reserved for a particular buyer.
- <b>Temporal specificity:</b> a service or delivery is valuable only at a particular time.

Imagine a supplier buying a specialised machine only to serve one customer. After the investment is made, the customer may have more bargaining power because the supplier cannot easily use the machine elsewhere. This is the <b>hold-up problem</b>.

Possible safeguards include long-term contracts, shared investment, minimum-purchase commitments, alliances, alternative suppliers or vertical integration. Integration is one possible response, not an automatic requirement.

### B. Uncertainty

Uncertainty may concern future demand, technology, quality, regulation, delivery volumes or the behaviour of the other party. Contracts cannot specify every future event, so the parties need ways to adapt and renegotiate.

<b>Important qualification:</b> uncertainty alone does not always mean a firm should integrate. Its effect depends on supplier alternatives, asset specificity, measurability and the cost of adapting contracts.

### C. Frequency

A one-time purchase may not justify building an internal team or an elaborate relationship. Repeated transactions can justify investing in shared routines, specialist knowledge, monitoring systems and stable supplier relationships.

### D. Measurability and complexity

If output is standardised and easy to inspect, a buyer can compare suppliers and enforce service levels more easily. If quality is hard to measure, work is highly interdependent or output depends on tacit knowledge, monitoring and contracting become harder.

## 5. A practical decision framework

1. Define the activity and required output, quality, volume, timing, security and service level.
2. Identify real internal capabilities and credible external suppliers.
3. Estimate lifecycle cost for make, buy and ally options.
4. Assess asset specificity, uncertainty, frequency, measurability and interdependence.
5. Check strategic importance, innovation, learning, data/IP control, resilience and supplier competition.
6. Design safeguards: service-level agreements, audit rights, data controls, change-control processes, exit clauses and dual sourcing.
7. Stress-test supplier failure, demand changes, technology changes, inflation and renegotiation.

| Situation | Likely arrangement to examine | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Standard output, many suppliers, easy measurement, low switching cost | Buy | Competition and supplier specialisation can help |
| Specific investment, dependence and difficult adaptation | Make or safeguarded hybrid | May reduce hold-up and renegotiation exposure |
| Specialist capability unavailable internally but coordination is ongoing | Ally or long-term contract | Combines outside expertise with continuity |
| Core knowledge, sensitive data or rapid product iteration | Often make or tightly governed hybrid | Can protect learning and control |

These are starting hypotheses, not automatic rules.

## 6. What empirical research says

### Meta-analysis of 200 studies

Geyskens, Steenkamp and Kumar's 2006 <i>Academy of Management Journal</i> paper synthesised <b>200 empirical papers, 209 independent samples, 557 correlations and a total sample size of 91,006</b>. It found broad support for transaction-cost theory in make-versus-buy and ally-versus-buy choices. It also found that governance arrangements aligned with transaction characteristics were associated with enhanced performance.

A crucial qualification: the study did <b>not</b> find evidence that asset specificity had stronger predictive power than uncertainty. This cautions against treating one factor as the universal master variable. [Article PDF](https://josephmahoney.web.illinois.edu/BADM504_Fall%202019/Geyskens%20et%20al.%202006.pdf)

### IT outsourcing: evidence from 335 firms

Aubert, Rivard and Patry's 2004 study tested a transaction-cost model of IT outsourcing using survey data from <b>335 firms</b>. Its indexed abstract reports that uncertainty was the major deterrent to outsourcing, while the level of technical skills was the most important reason to outsource.

Interpretation: specialist technical capability may be valuable to obtain externally, but uncertainty can make the relationship difficult to specify and manage. This is an IT-operations study, not a universal rule for every industry. [Study](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0378720603001423)

### UK engineering: 178 inputs

Lyons's 1995 study examined <b>178 inputs from UK engineering firms</b>. The indexed abstract reports that buying specialised inputs was more likely when production technology was non-specific, but only where scale or scope economies existed. Those effects were much weaker when specific assets were present.

This is a sector-specific finding, not an estimate for all countries or industries. [Study](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/016726819400070U)

### Why caution is necessary

Peter Klein's 2004 review explains that internal organisation has costs too—including information flow, incentives, monitoring and performance evaluation. It also highlights measurement problems, sample selection, alternative explanations and the difficulty of inferring causality from observed integration choices. [Review](https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=0a51948d869da8f5d1771f5293d1a1e7c59cc995&repid=rep1&type=pdf)

## 7. A modern example: an Indian AI product company

Imagine an Indian AI startup building a customer-facing product with a sensitive data pipeline.

- <b>Buy:</b> use an established cloud provider if infrastructure is standard, suppliers are credible, service levels are measurable and switching is manageable.
- <b>Make:</b> keep core architecture, proprietary data logic and security-sensitive engineering inside if they create firm-specific knowledge and require rapid coordination.
- <b>Ally:</b> retain architecture and security ownership internally while using external specialists for infrastructure, audits or selected components under strong service, audit, security and exit terms.

The same company can make some activities, buy others and ally for a third group. The correct unit of analysis is the activity or transaction, not the company as a whole.

## 8. Connection to jobs and salaries

- Making internally may create roles in engineering, operations, quality, security, procurement and management.
- Buying externally may move production work to supplier firms while creating buyer-side roles in vendor management, integration, security and quality.
- Alliances may create joint teams and coordination roles across organisations.

Outsourcing does not necessarily mean a task disappears from the economy. It may move to another employer, location or employment arrangement. Nor does governance choice mechanically set an individual's salary. Pay also depends on labour supply and demand, bargaining, employer economics, skills, institutions and geography.

## 9. Part 2 conclusion

A make-or-buy decision is a governance decision, not merely a price comparison. Firms should compare internal production, external procurement and hybrid alliances over the full lifecycle. Production costs, transaction costs, asset specificity, uncertainty, frequency, capability, innovation, switching, resilience and control all matter.

<b>Simple takeaway:</b> cheapest quotation does not necessarily mean cheapest overall option. Choose the arrangement that delivers the best total value with manageable risk.

# Stage 2 — Theory of the Firm

## Part 3: Ownership, Control and Agency Problems Inside Firms

<b>Research date:</b> 13 November 2026<br /><b>Status:</b> Research and source audit complete; full GitHub research-library integration pending.

## 1. The central question

Why can the people who own a company, the people who control its decisions and the people who perform its work have different interests? And how does the company manage those differences?

A company may have shareholders who provide capital, a board that supervises, executives who make strategic decisions, managers who direct teams and employees who perform the work. These roles overlap in small firms but become separated in larger organisations.

The separation can create value: owners can supply capital while specialist managers contribute expertise. But it also creates a problem: the person making a decision may not bear all its financial consequences.

## 2. Principal and agent: the basic model

- <b>Principal:</b> the party delegating a task, decision or authority.
- <b>Agent:</b> the party entrusted to act or decide on the principal's behalf.
- <b>Agency relationship:</b> a relationship involving delegated decision-making authority.
- <b>Information asymmetry:</b> one party knows more about actions, effort, quality or risks than the other.
- <b>Moral hazard:</b> actions after an agreement are difficult to observe, so incentives may diverge.
- <b>Adverse selection:</b> before an agreement, one party has private information about its type, quality or risk.

Agency theory does not say every manager is dishonest. Different incentives, risk preferences, information and time horizons can create conflict even when everyone is acting legally and believes they are making sensible decisions.

## 3. Why ownership and control become separated

In a small owner-operated shop, the owner may invest money, make decisions, supervise workers and bear the financial consequences.

In a large company, thousands of investors may own shares but cannot manage everyday operations. They delegate to directors and professional managers. This specialisation allows large organisations to access capital and management expertise, but shareholders cannot personally observe every decision.

Jensen and Meckling (1976) connect agency theory, property rights and finance to explain ownership structure and agency costs. Fama and Jensen (1983) explain why organisations separate decision management from decision control.

## 4. The three components of agency costs

Jensen and Meckling's conceptual decomposition is:

<b>Total agency cost = monitoring expenditure + bonding expenditure + residual loss</b>

- <b>Monitoring costs:</b> what the principal spends on reports, audits, supervision, evaluation and controls.
- <b>Bonding costs:</b> what the agent spends to credibly commit to behaviour, provide assurance or accept constraints.
- <b>Residual loss:</b> the value lost because interests still cannot be perfectly aligned after monitoring and bonding.

This is a conceptual model, not a universal accounting formula. Perfect alignment is usually too expensive or impossible. Firms choose a balance between control costs and the remaining risk of misalignment.

## 5. Decision rights inside a firm

Fama and Jensen (1983) distinguish four functions:

1. <b>Decision initiation:</b> propose and develop an option.
2. <b>Decision ratification:</b> approve or authorise the proposal.
3. <b>Decision implementation:</b> execute the approved action.
4. <b>Decision monitoring:</b> evaluate performance and compliance.

Their key idea is that organisations can separate decision management (initiation and implementation) from decision control (ratification and monitoring). This can reduce conflicts when decision-makers do not bear the full wealth effects of their decisions.

These are analytical functions, not necessarily four separate job titles. A founder in a small company may perform all four; a large company may allocate them across teams, executives, boards and committees.

## 6. Four important agency conflicts

### A. Owners versus managers — vertical agency problem

Managers may prefer private benefits, lower effort, prestige, job security, empire-building or excessive diversification over owners' risk-adjusted returns.

Example: a CEO pursues a costly acquisition that increases company size and personal status but has weak expected returns.

### B. Controlling versus minority shareholders — horizontal agency problem

A controlling owner may direct related-party transactions, asset transfers or other decisions that benefit the controller but harm minority investors.

Example: a listed company buys services from a promoter-linked entity on terms that are not demonstrably fair.

This is especially important in economies where ownership is concentrated. The classic dispersed-shareholder versus professional-manager problem is not the only agency problem.

### C. Shareholders versus creditors

After debt is issued, shareholders may favour riskier projects because equity receives much of the upside while creditors bear part of the downside. Alternatively, shareholders may avoid a positive project if much of its gain would go to existing lenders.

Example: a highly leveraged company shifts toward a volatile project after borrowing.

### D. Managers versus employees, or delegating managers versus teams

A manager may reward visible short-term output while employees know that quality, safety, teamwork and long-term value are harder to measure.

Example: a call centre rewards call speed, so workers have an incentive to end calls quickly rather than solve customer problems.

## 7. How firms try to manage agency problems

| Mechanism | How it helps | Important trade-off |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Salary, performance pay, profit sharing, equity | Connects some rewards to company outcomes | Metrics can be gamed; short-term targets may damage long-term value |
| Reporting, audit and internal controls | Reduces information gaps and detects problems | Monitoring costs money and can create bureaucracy |
| Independent boards and committees | Adds challenge, approval and oversight | Independence on paper does not guarantee judgement or expertise |
| Large shareholders and shareholder voting | Strengthens monitoring and makes control contestable | A controlling shareholder may also exploit minorities |
| Debt covenants and contractual limits | Constrains risk-shifting and asset transfers | Tight restrictions can block beneficial investment |
| Ethics, whistleblowing and professional norms | Supports trustworthy conduct where contracts are incomplete | Culture statements without enforcement can be symbolic |

### Better incentive design

- Reward outcomes the agent can materially influence.
- Use several measures when quality, safety, cooperation and long-term effects matter.
- Adjust for external shocks outside the agent's control.
- Balance performance pay with professional standards and judgement.
- Check unintended consequences; people adapt to the metrics used to evaluate them.

More monitoring or stronger performance pay is not always better. Incentives can shift risk onto workers, encourage gaming or reward measurable activity instead of meaningful outcomes.

## 8. What empirical research says

### Foundational theory — Jensen and Meckling (1976)

Their <i>Journal of Financial Economics</i> paper defines agency costs and analyses conflicts created by outside equity and debt. It is a theoretical framework, not a universal estimate of agency costs across firms. [Publisher record](<https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-405X(76)90026-X>)

### Agency theory review — Eisenhardt (1989)

Eisenhardt reviews the theory and empirical work, highlighting its insight into information systems, outcome uncertainty, incentives and risk. She also describes it as controversial and recommends using it alongside complementary perspectives rather than as the only explanation of organisations. [Review](https://doi.org/10.5465/amr.1989.4279003)

### Governance mechanisms are interdependent — Agrawal and Knoeber (1996)

This empirical study examines seven mechanisms: insider, institutional and blockholder shareholdings; outside directors; debt policy; the managerial labour market; and the market for corporate control. The authors find that mechanisms are interdependent. Results for individual mechanisms can change when several are analysed together or with simultaneous systems.

<b>Lesson:</b> it is misleading to claim that one isolated governance mechanism automatically improves firm performance. [Full-text copy](https://bpb-us-e2.wpmucdn.com/sites.ua.edu/dist/4/284/files/2024/10/jfqa96.pdf)

### Vertical and horizontal conflicts — UK evidence from more than 42,000 firms

Gogineni, Linn and Yadav's article (published online 2021; journal issue June 2022) uses more than <b>42,000 UK public and private firms</b> and <b>109,534 firm-year observations</b>. Its robustness tests use more than 200,000 firms and 500,000 firm-year observations.

The study distinguishes:

- vertical conflicts between owners and managers;
- horizontal conflicts between controlling and minority shareholders.

It reports that horizontal agency costs can be substantial in private firms, that a second large shareholder is associated with lower horizontal agency costs, and that the joint presence of vertical and horizontal problems can amplify measured costs.

<b>Important limitation:</b> these are UK data and proxy-based estimates. They are not direct estimates for India and should not be transferred mechanically to Indian companies. The authors' GDP-equivalent calculations are not automatically extrapolatable to other economies. [Open-access article](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0022109021000363)

### India-focused review — Panda and Leepsa (2017)

This review discusses conflicts arising from ownership-control separation, different risk preferences, information asymmetry and moral hazard. It considers managerial ownership, independent boards and committees as possible mechanisms, while the broader evidence shows that outcomes depend on context. [Review](https://doi.org/10.1177/0974686217701467)

## 9. India: promoters, minority shareholders and related-party transactions

India's corporate governance discussion must consider promoter and controlling-shareholder influence as well as the classic dispersed-shareholder versus manager conflict.

SEBI's Committee on Corporate Governance report (5 October 2017) discussed promoter/controlling shareholder conflicts and the protection of minority shareholders. It warned that formal compliance with rules does not necessarily mean their underlying purpose is being followed in practice. [SEBI report](https://www.sebi.gov.in/sebi_data/attachdocs/oct-2017/1509102194616.pdf)

A later SEBI circular dated <b>14 February 2025</b>, effective <b>1 April 2025</b>, specifies minimum information for audit committee and shareholder review of related-party transactions under LODR Regulation 23(2)–(4). Material related-party transactions require shareholder approval as specified in the regulations. [SEBI circular](https://www.sebi.gov.in/sebi_data/attachdocs/feb-2025/1739550952629.pdf)

These rules are safeguards, not proof that conflicts cannot occur. Actual compliance depends on current amended regulations and the facts of each transaction.

## 10. A technology-company example

Imagine an Indian technology company growing quickly.

- Founders and investors own the company.
- The board supervises major decisions.
- The CEO leads the business.
- Product, engineering, sales and finance managers control budgets and teams.
- Employees implement the work.

Potential conflicts:

1. The CEO may prefer expansion, prestige or acquisitions even if risk-adjusted returns are weak.
2. A product manager may optimise feature count or delivery speed because those are visible, while reliability and user trust are harder to measure.
3. A founder-promoter may direct a supplier contract toward a related entity, creating a conflict for outside investors.

Possible controls:

- Board approval for major investments and acquisitions.
- Independent audit and conflict review.
- Documented delegation limits and spending authority.
- Balanced product metrics including reliability and customer outcomes.
- Related-party transaction disclosure and disinterested review where required.
- Whistleblower and escalation channels.

<b>Lesson:</b> good governance combines decision rights, information, incentives, monitoring and accountability. One metric or one independent director cannot solve every conflict.

## 11. Connection to jobs and salaries

Employment is also a principal-agent relationship: an organisation delegates tasks to workers, but it cannot observe every action, effort or quality dimension.

Supervision, team norms, professional ethics, output measures and worker autonomy are different ways to organise work. Performance pay can align some interests, but it can also shift risk onto workers or reward easy-to-measure output over quality.

Ownership and control influence who decides what work is done, how it is measured, how risks are allocated and how the surplus is distributed. But wages are also shaped by labour markets, bargaining, institutions, skill scarcity, discrimination and law. Agency theory alone cannot explain pay.

## 12. Limitations and counterarguments

- The model can oversimplify firms by assuming one principal with one coherent objective.
- Different risk preferences or time horizons do not automatically imply misconduct.
- Monitoring is costly and can reduce trust, autonomy and innovation.
- Performance metrics are incomplete and can be gamed.
- Equity incentives may align managers with shareholders but can encourage excessive risk-taking or short-termism.
- Board independence on paper does not guarantee independent judgement or expertise.
- Concentrated ownership can reduce owner-manager conflict while increasing controlling-minority conflict.
- Governance studies face endogeneity and selection: firms choose structures in response to their circumstances.
- UK or US findings cannot be mechanically transferred to India or other legal systems.

## 13. Part 3 conclusion

Firms separate ownership, decision-making and work because specialisation and access to capital make delegation valuable. Delegation creates agency problems when information, incentives, risk and control are imperfectly aligned. The resulting costs include monitoring, bonding and residual loss.

Good governance does not mean maximising surveillance or performance pay. It means combining clear decision rights, reliable information, proportionate monitoring, incentives that avoid gaming, independent challenge and accountability suited to the firm's ownership structure and legal context.

<b>Simple takeaway:</b> the people who own a company, run it and do its work may be different people. Good governance makes sure power, information, incentives and accountability stay reasonably aligned.
